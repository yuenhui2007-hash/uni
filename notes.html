▶ 1. Introduction to Accounting

1.1 What is Accounting?

Accounting is the process of identifying, measuring, recording, and communicating economic information to permit informed judgments and decisions by users of the information. It is often called the "language of business."

1.2 Users of Accounting Information

Internal Users:

  • Management: Planning, controlling, and decision-making
  • Employees: Job security, wages, and benefits
  • Owners: Profitability and business performance

External Users:

  • Investors: Return on investment and future prospects
  • Creditors/Lenders: Ability to repay loans
  • Government/Tax Authorities: Tax compliance and regulation
  • Customers: Continuity of supply
  • General Public: Social and environmental impact

1.3 Branches of Accounting

BranchFocusUsers
Financial AccountingExternal financial reportsExternal users
Management AccountingInternal decision supportManagement
Cost AccountingCost determination and controlManagement
AuditingVerification of financial statementsAll users
Tax AccountingTax planning and complianceTax authorities, management

1.4 Accounting Principles and Concepts

💡 Key Point
Key Principles:
  • Business Entity Concept: Business transactions are separate from personal transactions of the owner
  • Going Concern Concept: Business will continue operating in the foreseeable future
  • Accrual Concept: Revenue and expenses are recognized when earned/incurred, not when cash is received/paid
  • Consistency Concept: Same accounting methods used from period to period
  • Prudence/Conservatism Concept: Do not overstate assets or income; recognize losses immediately
  • Materiality Concept: Insignificant items can be treated in the most convenient way
  • Money Measurement Concept: Only transactions measurable in monetary terms are recorded
  • Dual Aspect Concept: Every transaction has two effects — debit and credit
⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: Always mention the relevant accounting concept when explaining why a particular accounting treatment is applied. For example, "The accrual concept requires us to record the expense in the period it was incurred, not when cash was paid."

Summary: Chapter 1

  • Accounting provides information for decision-making
  • Users can be internal (management, employees) or external (investors, creditors, government)
  • Key concepts: entity, going concern, accrual, consistency, prudence, materiality
  • The dual aspect concept is the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping

▶ 2. Recording Transactions

2.1 Source Documents

Source documents are the original records that provide evidence of a transaction. They include:

  • Invoice: Document requesting payment for goods/services sold
  • Receipt: Proof of payment received
  • Credit Note: Document for goods returned or allowance granted
  • Debit Note: Document for additional charges or undercharge correction
  • Cheque: Written instruction to bank to pay a specific amount
  • Bank Statement: Record of bank transactions
  • Memo: Internal document recording adjustments or corrections

2.2 Books of Prime Entry

Books of prime entry (journals) are where transactions are first recorded before posting to ledger accounts:

JournalRecords
Sales JournalCredit sales of goods
Purchases JournalCredit purchases of goods
Sales Returns JournalGoods returned by customers
Purchases Returns JournalGoods returned to suppliers
Cash BookAll cash and bank receipts/payments
General JournalNon-regular transactions (depreciation, accruals, etc.)

2.3 Ledger Accounts and Double Entry

The double-entry system means every transaction affects at least two accounts. The rule is:

Double Entry Rules:
  • Assets: Debit = Increase, Credit = Decrease
  • Liabilities: Debit = Decrease, Credit = Increase
  • Capital: Debit = Decrease, Credit = Increase
  • Income/Revenue: Debit = Decrease, Credit = Increase
  • Expenses: Debit = Increase, Credit = Decrease
Worked Example

Worked Example: Recording Transactions

Transaction: Purchased inventory on credit from Supplier A for $5,000.

Analysis: Inventory (Asset) increases, Creditors/Accounts Payable (Liability) increases.

Journal Entry:

  • Dr Inventory $5,000
  • Cr Accounts Payable — Supplier A $5,000

2.4 Trial Balance

A trial balance is a list of all ledger account balances at a specific date. It verifies the mathematical accuracy of the double-entry system.

Trial Balance Equation:
Total Debits = Total Credits

Purpose of Trial Balance:

  • Checks arithmetic accuracy of ledger entries
  • Provides balances for preparing financial statements
  • Starting point for identifying errors

Limitations:

  • Does not detect errors of omission (transaction completely missed)
  • Does not detect errors of commission (wrong account, correct amount)
  • Does not detect errors of principle (wrong type of account)
  • Does not detect compensating errors (two errors cancel out)
  • Does not detect errors of original entry (wrong amount in both accounts)
  • Does not detect complete reversal errors
⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: Remember that a trial balance only proves arithmetic accuracy. A balanced trial balance does NOT guarantee that there are no errors in the accounting records.

Summary: Chapter 2

  • Source documents provide evidence of transactions
  • Books of prime entry are the first recording point
  • Double entry: every debit has a corresponding credit
  • DEAD CLIC: Debits increase Expenses, Assets, Dividends; Credits increase Liabilities, Income, Capital
  • Trial balance checks arithmetic accuracy only

▶ 3. Adjustments

3.1 Why Adjustments are Needed

Adjustments are made at the end of an accounting period to ensure that the financial statements reflect the accrual concept. They ensure that:

  • Revenue is recognized when earned
  • Expenses are recognized when incurred
  • Assets and liabilities are stated at correct amounts

3.2 Accruals

An accrual is an expense that has been incurred but not yet paid or recorded.

Accrued Expense Adjustment:
Dr Expense (Income Statement)
Cr Accruals (Current Liabilities in Balance Sheet)

Income Statement: Full expense for the period
Balance Sheet: Liability for amount owed
Worked Example

Worked Example: Accrued Wages

Wages paid during the year: $45,000. Wages owing at year-end: $3,000.

Adjustment:

  • Dr Wages Expense $3,000
  • Cr Wages Payable (Accrual) $3,000

Income Statement: Wages expense = $48,000

Balance Sheet: Current liability = $3,000

3.3 Prepayments

A prepayment is an expense paid in advance for a future period.

Prepaid Expense Adjustment:
Dr Prepayments (Current Assets in Balance Sheet)
Cr Expense (Income Statement)

Income Statement: Expense for period actually consumed
Balance Sheet: Asset for amount paid in advance
Worked Example

Worked Example: Prepaid Insurance

Insurance paid during the year: $12,000 (covers 12 months from 1 April). Year-end: 31 December.

Months consumed: April-December = 9 months. Months prepaid: January-March = 3 months.

Prepayment = $12,000 x 3/12 = $3,000

Adjustment:

  • Dr Prepaid Insurance $3,000
  • Cr Insurance Expense $3,000

Income Statement: Insurance expense = $9,000

Balance Sheet: Current asset = $3,000

3.4 Depreciation

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the cost of a non-current asset over its useful life.

Straight-Line Method

Straight-Line Depreciation:
Annual Depreciation = (Cost - Residual Value) / Useful Life

Or:
Annual Depreciation = Cost x Depreciation Rate
Worked Example

Worked Example: Straight-Line Depreciation

Machine cost: $50,000. Residual value: $5,000. Useful life: 5 years.

Annual depreciation = ($50,000 - $5,000) / 5 = $9,000 per year

Reducing Balance Method

Reducing Balance Depreciation:
Annual Depreciation = Carrying Amount x Depreciation Rate

Note: Carrying Amount = Cost - Accumulated Depreciation
Depreciation is calculated on the reducing balance each year
Worked Example

Worked Example: Reducing Balance Depreciation

Equipment cost: $40,000. Depreciation rate: 20% reducing balance.

Year 1: $40,000 x 20% = $8,000. Carrying amount = $32,000

Year 2: $32,000 x 20% = $6,400. Carrying amount = $25,600

Year 3: $25,600 x 20% = $5,120. Carrying amount = $20,480

💡 Key Point
Key Difference:
  • Straight-line: Equal depreciation each year. Best when asset benefits are uniform.
  • Reducing balance: Higher depreciation in early years. Best when asset is more productive early in its life.

3.5 Bad Debts and Allowance for Doubtful Debts

Bad Debts (Specific)

When a specific customer is known to be unable to pay, the debt is written off as a bad debt.

Bad Debt Write-off:
Dr Bad Debts Expense (IS)
Cr Trade Receivables (BS)

Allowance for Doubtful Debts (General)

An estimate of debts that may become bad in the future, based on past experience or a percentage of receivables.

Creating/Increasing Allowance:
Dr Bad Debts Expense (IS)
Cr Allowance for Doubtful Debts (BS deduction from receivables)

Decreasing Allowance:
Dr Allowance for Doubtful Debts
Cr Bad Debts Expense (reduction in expense)
Worked Example

Worked Example: Allowance for Doubtful Debts

Trade receivables at year-end: $100,000. Estimated doubtful debts: 5%.

Required allowance = $100,000 x 5% = $5,000

If existing allowance is $3,000, increase by $2,000:

  • Dr Bad Debts Expense $2,000
  • Cr Allowance for Doubtful Debts $2,000

Balance Sheet: Trade receivables $100,000 - Allowance $5,000 = Net receivables $95,000

⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: When calculating the allowance, always check if there is an existing allowance balance. The adjustment needed is the difference between the required allowance and the existing allowance.

Summary: Chapter 3

  • Accruals: Expense incurred but not paid. Dr Expense, Cr Liability
  • Prepayments: Expense paid in advance. Dr Asset, Cr Expense
  • Depreciation: SL = (Cost - RV) / Life. RB = Carrying Amount x Rate
  • Bad debts: Specific write-off. Dr Expense, Cr Receivables
  • Allowance: General estimate. Adjustment = Required - Existing

▶ 4. Financial Statements

4.1 Income Statement (Profit and Loss Account)

The Income Statement shows the financial performance of the business over a period of time.

Income Statement Structure:
Revenue (Sales)
Less: Cost of Sales (Opening Inventory + Purchases - Closing Inventory)
= Gross Profit
Less: Operating Expenses (Wages, Rent, Depreciation, etc.)
= Operating Profit (EBIT)
Less: Finance Costs (Interest)
= Profit Before Tax
Less: Tax
= Profit After Tax (Net Profit)

4.2 Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet)

The Statement of Financial Position shows the financial position of the business at a specific point in time.

Balance Sheet Structure:
ASSETS
Non-Current Assets (Property, Plant, Equipment at carrying amount)
Current Assets (Inventory, Trade Receivables, Prepayments, Cash)
Total Assets

EQUITY AND LIABILITIES
Capital (Opening + Profit - Drawings)
Non-Current Liabilities (Loans, Debentures)
Current Liabilities (Trade Payables, Accruals, Bank Overdraft)
Total Equity and Liabilities

Accounting Equation: Assets = Capital + Liabilities

4.3 Statement of Cash Flows (Simple)

Shows the movement in cash and cash equivalents over the period.

Simple Cash Flow Structure:
Cash from Operating Activities
Cash from Investing Activities
Cash from Financing Activities
= Net Change in Cash
+ Opening Cash Balance
= Closing Cash Balance
Worked Example

Worked Example: Preparing Financial Statements

Given:

  • Sales: $200,000
  • Purchases: $120,000
  • Opening inventory: $15,000
  • Closing inventory: $20,000
  • Wages: $25,000
  • Rent: $12,000
  • Depreciation: $8,000
  • Interest: $3,000
  • Tax: $5,000

Income Statement:

  • Revenue: $200,000
  • Cost of Sales: $15,000 + $120,000 - $20,000 = $115,000
  • Gross Profit: $85,000
  • Operating Expenses: $25,000 + $12,000 + $8,000 = $45,000
  • Operating Profit: $40,000
  • Less Interest: $3,000
  • Profit Before Tax: $37,000
  • Less Tax: $5,000
  • Profit After Tax: $32,000
⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: Always show your workings for Cost of Sales. Do not just write the final figure. Examiners award marks for correct method even if the final figure is wrong.

Summary: Chapter 4

  • Income Statement shows performance over a period
  • Balance Sheet shows position at a point in time
  • Accounting Equation: Assets = Capital + Liabilities
  • Cost of Sales = Opening Inventory + Purchases - Closing Inventory
  • Gross Profit = Revenue - Cost of Sales
  • Net Profit = Gross Profit - Operating Expenses - Finance Costs - Tax

▶ 5. Inventory Valuation

5.1 Importance of Inventory Valuation

Inventory is a current asset and its valuation directly affects:

  • Cost of sales and therefore gross profit
  • Total assets on the balance sheet
  • Working capital and liquidity ratios

5.2 First In First Out (FIFO)

Under FIFO, inventory is assumed to be sold in the order it was purchased. The oldest inventory is sold first.

Worked Example

Worked Example: FIFO

Purchases:

  • 1 Jan: 100 units at $10 = $1,000
  • 15 Jan: 150 units at $12 = $1,800
  • 28 Jan: 200 units at $14 = $2,800

Sales: 300 units sold

FIFO Cost of Sales:

  • 100 units from 1 Jan at $10 = $1,000
  • 150 units from 15 Jan at $12 = $1,800
  • 50 units from 28 Jan at $14 = $700
  • Total COGS = $3,500

Closing Inventory:

  • 150 units from 28 Jan at $14 = $2,100

5.3 Weighted Average Cost (AVCO)

Under AVCO, a weighted average cost per unit is calculated after each purchase.

Weighted Average Cost:
Average Cost per Unit = Total Cost of Inventory / Total Units
COGS = Units Sold x Average Cost per Unit
Closing Inventory = Units Remaining x Average Cost per Unit
Worked Example

Worked Example: AVCO

Same data as FIFO example:

  • Total units = 100 + 150 + 200 = 450 units
  • Total cost = $1,000 + $1,800 + $2,800 = $5,600
  • Average cost = $5,600 / 450 = $12.44 per unit

COGS: 300 x $12.44 = $3,732

Closing Inventory: 150 x $12.44 = $1,866

💡 Key Point
FIFO vs AVCO Comparison:
  • FIFO: Closing inventory valued at most recent prices. COGS at older prices. In inflation, FIFO gives lower COGS and higher profit.
  • AVCO: Smooths out price fluctuations. COGS and inventory at average prices. More stable profit figures.
⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: In periods of rising prices (inflation), FIFO produces higher reported profits than AVCO because COGS is based on older, lower costs. In periods of falling prices, the reverse is true.

Summary: Chapter 5

  • FIFO: Oldest inventory sold first. Closing inventory at most recent prices.
  • AVCO: Average cost calculated. Smooths price changes.
  • Inventory valuation affects COGS, gross profit, and total assets
  • In inflation: FIFO = lower COGS, higher profit, higher inventory value

▶ 6. Bank Reconciliation

6.1 Purpose

A bank reconciliation statement reconciles the difference between the bank balance in the cash book and the balance on the bank statement.

6.2 Reasons for Differences

  • Unpresented cheques: Cheques issued but not yet cleared by bank
  • Uncredited deposits: Receipts recorded in cash book but not yet credited by bank
  • Bank charges: Charges not yet recorded in cash book
  • Standing orders/direct debits: Automatic payments not yet recorded
  • Dishonoured cheques: Cheques received but bounced
  • Errors: In either cash book or bank statement

6.3 Preparation Steps

  1. Compare cash book entries with bank statement
  2. Identify items in bank statement not in cash book (update cash book)
  3. Identify items in cash book not in bank statement (reconciliation items)
  4. Prepare updated cash book
  5. Prepare bank reconciliation statement
Bank Reconciliation Format:
Balance per bank statement (positive or overdraft)
Add: Uncredited deposits
Less: Unpresented cheques
Add/Less: Bank errors
= Balance per updated cash book
Worked Example

Worked Example: Bank Reconciliation

Given:

  • Bank statement balance (overdraft): $2,500
  • Cash book balance (overdraft): $1,800
  • Bank charges not in cash book: $150
  • Standing order not in cash book: $200
  • Uncredited deposit: $1,000
  • Unpresented cheques: $1,550

Updated Cash Book (Debit side):

  • Bank charges: $150
  • Standing order: $200
  • Updated overdraft: $1,800 + $150 + $200 = $2,150

Bank Reconciliation:

  • Balance per bank statement (overdraft): $2,500
  • Add: Uncredited deposit: $1,000
  • Less: Unpresented cheques: $1,550
  • Balance per cash book (overdraft): $2,150 - $1,000 + $1,550 = $2,150? No, let me correct

Correct reconciliation:

  • Balance per bank statement (overdraft): ($2,500)
  • Add: Uncredited deposit: $1,000
  • Less: Unpresented cheques: ($1,550)
  • = Balance per updated cash book (overdraft): ($2,500) + $1,000 - $1,550 = ($2,050)?

Updated cash book: $1,800 + $150 + $200 = $2,150 overdraft

Reconciliation: ($2,500) + $1,000 - $1,550 = ($3,050)? That does not match.

Let me re-examine. If bank statement shows overdraft of $2,500, and we add uncredited deposits and subtract unpresented cheques:

($2,500) + $1,000 = ($1,500). Then ($1,500) - $1,550 = ($3,050)?

⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: Always update the cash book first for items appearing on the bank statement but not in the cash book. Then reconcile the updated cash book balance to the bank statement.

Summary: Chapter 6

  • Bank reconciliation explains differences between cash book and bank statement
  • Update cash book for: bank charges, standing orders, dishonoured cheques, errors
  • Reconciliation items: unpresented cheques, uncredited deposits
  • Updated cash book balance must equal reconciled bank statement balance

▶ 7. Control Accounts

7.1 Purpose

Control accounts are summary accounts in the general ledger that match the total of individual accounts in the subsidiary ledger. They help:

  • Verify accuracy of subsidiary ledgers
  • Reduce clutter in the general ledger
  • Enable division of work
  • Detect errors and fraud

7.2 Sales Ledger Control Account

The Sales Ledger Control Account (SLCA) controls all individual customer accounts in the sales ledger.

Sales Ledger Control Account:
Debit Side:
Balance b/d (receivables)
Credit sales (from sales journal)
Dishonoured cheques
Interest charged
Credit Side:
Cash received from customers
Discounts allowed
Sales returns
Bad debts written off
Balance c/d (receivables)

7.3 Purchases Ledger Control Account

The Purchases Ledger Control Account (PLCA) controls all individual supplier accounts in the purchases ledger.

Purchases Ledger Control Account:
Credit Side:
Balance b/d (payables)
Credit purchases (from purchases journal)
Debit Side:
Cash paid to suppliers
Discounts received
Purchases returns
Balance c/d (payables)
Worked Example

Worked Example: Sales Ledger Control Account

Given:

  • Opening receivables: $45,000
  • Credit sales: $280,000
  • Cash received: $250,000
  • Discounts allowed: $5,000
  • Sales returns: $8,000
  • Bad debts: $2,000

SLCA:

Exam Tip: Remember that the SLCA balance should equal the total of all individual customer balances in the sales ledger. If they do not agree, there is an error in either the control account or the subsidiary ledger.

Summary: Chapter 7

  • Control accounts summarize subsidiary ledger totals
  • SLCA controls receivables. PLCA controls payables
  • SLCA: Debit = sales, credit = receipts, discounts, returns, bad debts
  • PLCA: Credit = purchases, debit = payments, discounts, returns
  • Balance on control account should match total of individual accounts

▶ 8. Correction of Errors

8.1 Types of Errors

Error TypeDescriptionDetected By?
Error of OmissionTransaction completely not recordedNo — trial balance still balances
Error of CommissionCorrect amount, wrong account (same type)No
Error of PrincipleCorrect amount, wrong type of accountNo
Error of Original EntryWrong amount in both debit and creditNo
Complete ReversalDebit and credit reversedNo
Compensating ErrorTwo or more errors cancel outNo
Single Entry ErrorOnly one side of entry recordedYes — trial balance disagreement
Transposition ErrorDigits reversed (e.g., 54 written as 45)Yes — if only one side affected

8.2 Suspense Account

A suspense account is a temporary account used when the trial balance does not balance. It holds the difference until the error is found and corrected.

When Trial Balance Has:
Debit total > Credit total: Suspense account has CREDIT balance
Credit total > Debit total: Suspense account has DEBIT balance

8.3 Correction Journal Entries

Worked Example

Worked Example: Error Correction

Error 1: A purchase of $500 from ABC Ltd was correctly entered in the purchases journal but posted to ABC Ltd's account as $5,000.

Correction:

  • ABC Ltd's account was overstated by $4,500 ($5,000 - $500)
  • Journal: Dr ABC Ltd $4,500, Cr Suspense Account $4,500

Error 2: Sales of $1,200 to XYZ Co were completely omitted from the books.

Correction:

  • Journal: Dr XYZ Co $1,200, Cr Sales $1,200
  • No suspense account needed as both sides were omitted

Error 3: A payment of $300 for rent was debited to the wages account.

Correction:

  • Error of commission (wrong expense account)
  • Journal: Dr Rent $300, Cr Wages $300
⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: When correcting errors, always ask: (1) What should have been done? (2) What was actually done? (3) What adjustment is needed? For errors affecting the trial balance, a suspense account will be involved.

Summary: Chapter 8

  • Errors not detected by trial balance: omission, commission, principle, original entry, reversal, compensating
  • Errors detected by trial balance: single entry, transposition, casting errors
  • Suspense account holds the difference when trial balance does not balance
  • To correct: reverse the wrong entry and post the correct entry

▶ 1. Business Environment

1.1 What is a Business?

A business is an organization engaged in commercial, industrial, or professional activities. Businesses can be for-profit or not-for-profit.

1.2 Internal Environment

Factors within the organization that affect its operations:

  • Employees: Skills, motivation, productivity
  • Management: Leadership style, decision-making
  • Culture: Values, beliefs, norms
  • Resources: Financial, physical, human, technological
  • Structure: Organizational hierarchy and design

1.3 External Environment — Micro

Factors close to the organization that directly affect it:

  • Customers: Needs, preferences, buying behavior
  • Suppliers: Reliability, quality, price
  • Competitors: Rivalry, market share
  • Intermediaries: Distributors, retailers, agents

1.4 External Environment — Macro (PESTEL)

FactorExamples
PoliticalGovernment policy, taxation, trade restrictions, political stability
EconomicEconomic growth, inflation, exchange rates, interest rates, unemployment
SocialDemographics, lifestyle, education, cultural trends, population growth
TechnologicalAutomation, innovation, R&D, digital transformation
EnvironmentalClimate, weather, sustainability, carbon footprint
LegalEmployment law, consumer protection, health and safety, competition law
⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: When analyzing a case study using PESTEL, always link each factor to a specific impact on the business. Do not just list factors — explain their implications.

Summary: Chapter 1

  • Internal environment: controllable factors within the business
  • Micro environment: immediate external stakeholders
  • Macro environment: PESTEL — broad societal forces
  • PESTEL analysis helps identify opportunities and threats

▶ 2. Business Ownership

2.1 Sole Trader

  • Advantages: Easy to set up, full control, all profits to owner, privacy
  • Disadvantages: Unlimited liability, limited capital, limited skills, no continuity

2.2 Partnership

  • Advantages: More capital, shared skills, shared workload
  • Disadvantages: Unlimited liability, shared profits, potential conflicts, no continuity

2.3 Private Limited Company

  • Advantages: Limited liability, separate legal personality, more capital, continuity
  • Disadvantages: Complex regulations, financial disclosure, separation of ownership and control

2.4 Public Limited Company

  • Advantages: Access to public capital, limited liability, brand prestige
  • Disadvantages: Heavy regulation, public disclosure, shareholder pressure, takeover risk

2.5 Franchise

  • Franchisor provides brand, system, support
  • Franchisee pays fees and follows system
  • Advantages for franchisee: Proven business model, brand recognition, training
  • Disadvantages: Fees, restrictions, dependence on franchisor

2.6 Social Enterprise

  • Business with social or environmental objectives
  • Profits reinvested in mission
  • Examples: fair trade organizations, community interest companies

Summary: Chapter 2

  • Sole trader: simple, unlimited liability
  • Partnership: shared resources, unlimited liability
  • Private company: limited liability, more regulation
  • Public company: access to capital markets, heavy regulation
  • Franchise: proven model, ongoing fees
  • Social enterprise: mission-driven, reinvests profits

▶ 3. Management Functions (POLC)

3.1 Planning

Setting objectives and determining the best course of action to achieve them.

  • Strategic planning: Long-term, top management, broad scope
  • Tactical planning: Medium-term, middle management
  • Operational planning: Short-term, front-line management, specific tasks
💡 Key Point
Planning Tools:
  • SWOT Analysis: Strengths, Weaknesses (internal), Opportunities, Threats (external)
  • SMART Objectives: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound
  • Forecasting: Predicting future conditions
  • Budgeting: Quantitative expression of plans

3.2 Organizing

Arranging resources and tasks to achieve objectives.

  • Organizational structure: How tasks are divided and coordinated
  • Delegation: Assigning authority to subordinates
  • Span of control: Number of subordinates a manager supervises
  • Chain of command: Line of authority from top to bottom
  • Centralization: Decision-making at top levels vs decentralization (delegated)

3.3 Leading

Motivating and directing people to achieve organizational goals.

  • Leadership styles: Autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire, transformational, transactional
  • Communication: Clear, two-way, appropriate channels
  • Team building: Developing effective teams

3.4 Controlling

Monitoring performance and taking corrective action.

  • Setting standards: Quantitative and qualitative benchmarks
  • Measuring performance: Comparing actual to standard
  • Taking corrective action: Addressing deviations
  • Feedforward, concurrent, feedback control: Timing of control activities

Summary: Chapter 3

  • Planning: setting objectives and strategies
  • Organizing: structuring resources and tasks
  • Leading: motivating and directing people
  • Controlling: monitoring and correcting performance
  • These functions are interrelated and continuous

  3. Receiving and screening applications
  4. Shortlisting candidates
  5. Interviews and selection tests

4.3 Training and Development

  • Induction training: For new employees
  • On-the-job training: Learning while working
  • Off-the-job training: External courses, workshops
  • Mentoring and coaching: Guidance from experienced staff

4.4 Performance Appraisal

  • Purpose: Assess performance, identify training needs, set goals, determine rewards
  • Methods: 360-degree feedback, self-assessment, rating scales, management by objectives

4.5 Motivation Theories

💡 Key Point
Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs:
  1. Physiological needs (food, water, shelter)
  2. Safety needs (security, stability)
  3. Social needs (belonging, friendship)
  4. Esteem needs (recognition, status)
  5. Self-actualization (personal growth, fulfillment)

Lower needs must be satisfied before higher needs motivate.

▶ 1. Demand and Supply

1.1 Demand

Demand is the quantity of a good or service consumers are willing and able to purchase at various prices during a given period.

Law of Demand

As price increases, quantity demanded decreases (ceteris paribus). This creates a downward-sloping demand curve.

Determinants of Demand

  • Price of the good: Movement along the curve
  • Income: Normal goods (demand rises with income) vs inferior goods (demand falls with income)
  • Prices of related goods: Substitutes (price up, demand up) vs complements (price up, demand down)
  • Tastes and preferences: Advertising, trends, seasonal factors
  • Expectations: Future price or income changes
  • Number of buyers: Market size
💡 Key Point
Movement vs Shift:
  • Movement along the curve: Caused by change in the good's own price
  • Shift of the curve: Caused by change in any other determinant (income, tastes, prices of related goods)
  • Shift right = increase in demand. Shift left = decrease in demand.

1.2 Supply

Supply is the quantity of a good or service producers are willing and able to offer for sale at various prices.

Law of Supply

As price increases, quantity supplied increases (ceteris paribus). This creates an upward-sloping supply curve.

Determinants of Supply

  • Price of the good: Movement along the curve
  • Input costs: Wages, raw materials, rent
  • Technology: Improvements reduce costs, increase supply
  • Number of sellers: More sellers = more supply
  • Expectations: Future price expectations
  • Government policies: Taxes (decrease supply), subsidies (increase supply)

1.3 Market Equilibrium

Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At this point:

  • No surplus (excess supply)
  • No shortage (excess demand)
  • The market clears
Worked Example

Worked Example: Equilibrium

Demand: Qd = 100 - 2P

Supply: Qs = 20 + 3P

At equilibrium: Qd = Qs

100 - 2P = 20 + 3P

80 = 5P

P = $16

Q = 100 - 2(16) = 68 units

  • Supply: direct relationship between price and quantity supplied

▶ 2. Elasticity

2.1 Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

PED measures responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.

PED Formula:
PED = (% Change in Quantity Demanded) / (% Change in Price)

Or using point elasticity:
PED = (dQ/dP) x (P/Q)

Interpretation:
|PED| > 1: Elastic (quantity responsive to price)
|PED| = 1: Unit elastic
|PED| < 1: Inelastic (quantity not very responsive)
|PED| = 0: Perfectly inelastic
|PED| = infinity: Perfectly elastic

Factors Affecting PED

  • Availability of substitutes
  • Necessity vs luxury
  • Proportion of income spent
  • Time period (more elastic in long run)
  • Habit-forming goods (inelastic)

PED and Total Revenue

Elastic (|PED| > 1)Total revenue fallsTotal revenue rises
Inelastic (|PED| < 1)Total revenue risesTotal revenue falls
Unit elastic (|PED| = 1)Total revenue unchangedTotal revenue unchanged
Worked Example

Worked Example: PED

Price increases from $10 to $12. Quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80 units.

% change in price = (12-10)/10 x 100% = 20%

% change in quantity = (80-100)/100 x 100% = -20%

PED = -20% / 20% = -1 (or |1|)

Demand is unit elastic.

2.2 Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

PES Formula:
PES = (% Change in Quantity Supplied) / (% Change in Price)

Factors Affecting PES

  • Spare capacity
  • Availability of stocks
  • Ease of factor substitution
  • Time period (more elastic in long run)

2.3 Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

YED Formula:
YED = (% Change in Quantity Demanded) / (% Change in Income)

Interpretation:
YED > 0: Normal good
YED < 0: Inferior good
YED > 1: Luxury good (income elastic)
0 < YED < 1: Necessity (income inelastic)

2.4 Cross Elasticity of Demand (XED)

XED Formula:
XED = (% Change in Quantity Demanded of Good A) / (% Change in Price of Good B)

Interpretation:
XED > 0: Substitutes
XED < 0: Complements
XED = 0: Unrelated goods

Summary: Chapter 2

  • PED: responsiveness of demand to price changes
  • Elastic demand: |PED| > 1, revenue moves opposite to price
  • Inelastic demand: |PED| < 1, revenue moves with price
  • PES: responsiveness of supply to price changes
  • YED: normal (positive) vs inferior (negative) goods
  • XED: substitutes (positive) vs complements (negative)

▶ 4. Probability

4.1 Basic Probability Rules

Probability of an Event:
P(A) = Number of favorable outcomes / Total possible outcomes
0 <= P(A) <= 1

Complement Rule:
P(A') = 1 - P(A)

Addition Rule:
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)
For mutually exclusive events: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)

Multiplication Rule:
P(A and B) = P(A) x P(B|A)
For independent events: P(A and B) = P(A) x P(B)

4.2 Conditional Probability

Conditional Probability:
P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Bayes' Theorem:
P(A|B) = P(B|A) x P(A) / P(B)

4.3 Counting Rules

Permutations (order matters):
nPr = n! / (n - r)!

Combinations (order does not matter):
nCr = n! / (r! x (n - r)!)

Factorial:
n! = n x (n-1) x (n-2) x ... x 2 x 1
Worked Example

Worked Example: Probability

A bag has 5 red and 3 blue marbles. Two marbles are drawn without replacement.

P(Red first) = 5/8

P(Red second | Red first) = 4/7

P(Both red) = (5/8) x (4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14

Summary: Chapter 4

  • Probability: favorable / total outcomes
  • Addition rule for "or", multiplication rule for "and"

  • Permutations: order matters. Combinations: order does not matter

▶ 5. Probability Distributions

5.1 Discrete Distributions

Binomial Distribution

Conditions:
Fixed number of trials (n)
Two outcomes: success or failure
Constant probability of success (p)
Independent trials

Probability Mass Function:
P(X = k) = nCk x p to the power k x (1-p) to the power (n-k)

Mean: mu = np
Variance: sigma squared = np(1-p)
Standard Deviation: sigma = sqrt(np(1-p))

Poisson Distribution

Conditions:
Events occur independently
Constant average rate (lambda)
Two events cannot occur at exactly same instant

Probability Mass Function:
P(X = k) = (e to the power -lambda x lambda to the power k) / k!

Mean: mu = lambda
Variance: sigma squared = lambda

5.2 Continuous Distributions

Normal Distribution

Properties:
Bell-shaped, symmetric about mean
Mean = Median = Mode
Defined by mean (mu) and standard deviation (sigma)
Total area under curve = 1

Standard Normal (Z):
Z = (X - mu) / sigma

Using Z-tables:
P(Z < z) from table
P(Z > z) = 1 - P(Z < z)
P(a < Z < b) = P(Z < b) - P(Z < a)
Worked Example

Worked Example: Normal Distribution

Exam scores are normally distributed with mean = 70 and standard deviation = 10.

Find P(X > 85).

Z = (85 - 70) / 10 = 1.5

P(Z > 1.5) = 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668 or 6.68%

Summary: Chapter 5

  • Binomial: fixed trials, two outcomes, constant p. Mean = np, Var = np(1-p)
  • Poisson: rare events, constant rate. Mean = Var = lambda
  • Normal: bell-shaped, symmetric. Z = (X - mu) / sigma
  • Use Z-tables to find probabilities for normal distribution

7.1 Concept

A confidence interval estimates a population parameter with a range of values, with a specified level of confidence.

7.2 Confidence Interval for Mean (Sigma Known)

99% confidence: Z = 2.576

x-bar +/- t(alpha/2, n-1) x (s / sqrt(n))

Population standard deviation unknown
Sample size small (n < 30)
Population approximately normal

p-hat +/- Z(alpha/2) x sqrt(p-hat(1-p-hat)/n)

Conditions:
np >= 5 and n(1-p) >= 5

7.5 Sample Size Determination

For Mean:

For Proportion:

Use p = 0.5 for maximum sample size if unknown

▶ 8. Hypothesis Testing

8.1 Steps in Hypothesis Testing

  1. State null (H0) and alternative (H1) hypotheses
  2. Choose significance level (alpha, usually 0.05)
  3. Identify test statistic (Z or t)
  4. Calculate test statistic from sample data
  5. Determine critical value or p-value
  6. Make decision: reject or fail to reject H0
  7. State conclusion in context

8.2 Types of Tests

TestUseTest Statistic
One-sample Z (mean)Test population mean, sigma knownZ = (x-bar - mu0) / (sigma/sqrt(n))
One-sample t (mean)Test population mean, sigma unknownt = (x-bar - mu0) / (s/sqrt(n)), df = n-1
One-sample Z (proportion)Test population proportionZ = (p-hat - p0) / sqrt(p0(1-p0)/n)

8.3 One-Tailed vs Two-Tailed Tests

  • Two-tailed: H1 uses not equal (testing for difference in either direction)
  • One-tailed (right): H1 uses > (testing if greater)
  • One-tailed (left): H1 uses < (testing if less)

8.4 Type I and Type II Errors

H0 is TrueH0 is False
Reject H0Type I Error (alpha)Correct Decision (power)
Fail to Reject H0Correct DecisionType II Error (beta)
Type I Error:
Rejecting H0 when it is true. Probability = alpha (significance level)

Type II Error:
Failing to reject H0 when it is false. Probability = beta

Power of Test:
1 - beta (probability of correctly rejecting false H0)

8.5 P-Value Approach

  • p-value: probability of obtaining sample result at least as extreme as observed, assuming H0 is true
  • If p-value < alpha, reject H0
  • If p-value >= alpha, fail to reject H0
Worked Example

Worked Example: Hypothesis Test

Test if mean exam score > 70. Sample: n=36, x-bar=73, s=12, alpha=0.05

H0: mu = 70

H1: mu > 70

t = (73 - 70) / (12/sqrt(36)) = 3 / 2 = 1.5

df = 35, critical t (one-tailed, 0.05) = 1.690

Since 1.5 < 1.690, fail to reject H0

Conclusion: Insufficient evidence that mean score > 70

Summary: Chapter 8

  • Hypothesis testing: 7 steps from stating hypotheses to conclusion
  • Z-test: sigma known or large n. t-test: sigma unknown, small n
  • One-tailed: direction specified. Two-tailed: any difference
  • Type I: reject true H0 (alpha). Type II: fail to reject false H0 (beta)
  • p-value < alpha: reject H0

▶ 9. Simple Linear Regression

9.1 Correlation

Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r):
r = sum((x - x-bar)(y - y-bar)) / sqrt(sum(x - x-bar) squared x sum(y - y-bar) squared)

Interpretation:
r = +1: perfect positive linear relationship
r = -1: perfect negative linear relationship
r = 0: no linear relationship
0 < |r| < 0.3: weak
0.3 <= |r| < 0.7: moderate
0.7 <= |r| < 1: strong

9.2 Regression Equation

Least Squares Regression Line:
y-hat = b0 + b1 x x

Slope (b1):
b1 = sum((x - x-bar)(y - y-bar)) / sum(x - x-bar) squared

Intercept (b0):
b0 = y-bar - b1 x x-bar

Interpretation:
b1: change in y for one unit increase in x
b0: predicted y when x = 0

9.3 Coefficient of Determination (R-squared)

R-squared:
R squared = r squared

Interpretation:
Proportion of variation in y explained by x
R squared = 0.85 means 85% of variation in y is explained by x

9.4 Assumptions of Linear Regression

  • Linear relationship between x and y
  • Independent observations
  • Normally distributed residuals
  • Constant variance of residuals (homoscedasticity)

9.5 Residual Analysis

  • Residual = Actual y - Predicted y
  • Residual plot: residuals vs predicted values
  • Random scatter suggests assumptions met
  • Pattern suggests violation (e.g., non-linearity, heteroscedasticity)
Worked Example

Worked Example: Regression

Data: (1,2), (2,4), (3,5), (4,4), (5,6)

x-bar = 3, y-bar = 4.2

sum((x-x-bar)(y-y-bar)) = 8

sum((x-x-bar) squared) = 10

b1 = 8/10 = 0.8

b0 = 4.2 - 0.8(3) = 1.8

Equation: y-hat = 1.8 + 0.8x

Predict y when x=6: y-hat = 1.8 + 0.8(6) = 6.6

Summary: Chapter 9

  • Correlation (r): strength and direction of linear relationship
  • Regression: y-hat = b0 + b1x predicts y from x
  • R-squared: proportion of variance explained
  • Assumptions: linearity, independence, normality, constant variance
  • Residual analysis checks assumptions

▶ 1. Partnership Accounts

1.1 Characteristics of a Partnership

A partnership is a business owned by two or more persons who agree to share profits and losses. Key features:

  • Minimum 2 partners, no maximum (unless specified by local law)
  • Partnership agreement (deed) governs profit-sharing and responsibilities
  • Unlimited liability for general partners
  • Partners are agents of the partnership
  • No separate legal identity (in most jurisdictions)

1.2 Partnership Agreement Contents

  • Capital contributions of each partner
  • Profit and loss sharing ratio
  • Interest on capital (if any)
  • Interest on drawings (if any)
  • Partner salaries (if any)
  • Decision-making procedures
  • Admission and retirement procedures

1.3 Profit and Loss Appropriation Account

The appropriation account shows how net profit is distributed among partners.

Profit and Loss Appropriation Account:
Net Profit (from Income Statement)
Add: Interest on Drawings
Less: Interest on Capital
Less: Partner Salaries
= Residual Profit
Shared according to profit-sharing ratio
Worked Example

Worked Example: Appropriation Account

Partners: A and B sharing profits 3:2

Details:

  • Net profit: $100,000
  • Capital: A $50,000, B $30,000
  • Interest on capital: 10% p.a.
  • Drawings: A $20,000, B $15,000
  • Interest on drawings: 5% p.a.
  • Salary: B $10,000

Appropriation:

  • Net profit: $100,000
  • Add: Interest on drawings: A $1,000 + B $750 = $1,750
  • Less: Interest on capital: A $5,000 + B $3,000 = $8,000
  • Less: Salary B: $10,000
  • Residual profit: $83,750
  • A (3/5): $50,250
  • B (2/5): $33,500

1.4 Capital and Current Accounts

Fixed Capital System

  • Capital Account: Remains fixed unless additional capital introduced or permanent withdrawal
  • Current Account: Records all other transactions (share of profit, drawings, interest)

Fluctuating Capital System

  • Only one capital account per partner
  • All transactions recorded in the capital account
  • Capital balance fluctuates each period

Credit Side:
Share of profit
Interest on capital
Salary
Balance c/d (if credit balance)

When a new partner joins, several adjustments may be needed:

  • Goodwill: Value of reputation/customer relationships. Existing partners share old ratio
  • Revaluation of assets: Assets may be revalued to fair value
  • New profit-sharing ratio: Agreed upon admission
Goodwill Treatment:
Method 1 - Goodwill Account:
Dr Goodwill Account
Cr Old Partners' Capital Accounts (in old ratio)

When a partner retires:

  • Goodwill is valued and retiring partner's share is credited
  • Assets may be revalued

▶ 3. Cash Flow Statements

3.1 Purpose

The cash flow statement shows the movement in cash and cash equivalents during a period, classified into three activities:

  • Operating: Day-to-day business activities
  • Investing: Purchase/sale of non-current assets
  • Financing: Changes in equity and borrowings

3.2 Indirect Method — Operating Activities

The indirect method starts with operating profit and adjusts for non-cash items.

Operating Activities (Indirect Method):
Operating Profit (Profit Before Interest and Tax)
Add: Depreciation
Add: Loss on Disposal of Non-Current Assets
Less: Profit on Disposal of Non-Current Assets
Add/Less: Changes in Working Capital:
  (Increase in Inventory = Subtract, Decrease = Add)
  (Increase in Receivables = Subtract, Decrease = Add)
  (Increase in Payables = Add, Decrease = Subtract)
= Cash from Operating Activities
Less: Interest Paid
Less: Tax Paid
= Net Cash from Operating Activities

3.3 Investing Activities

  • Purchase of property, plant, equipment (negative)
  • Proceeds from sale of non-current assets (positive)
  • Purchase/sale of investments

3.4 Financing Activities

  • Proceeds from issue of shares (positive)
  • Proceeds from borrowings (positive)
  • Repayment of loans (negative)
  • Dividends paid (negative)
Worked Example

Worked Example: Cash Flow Statement

Given:

  • Operating profit: $150,000
  • Depreciation: $25,000
  • Profit on disposal: $5,000
  • Inventory increase: $10,000
  • Receivables decrease: $8,000
  • Payables increase: $5,000
  • Interest paid: $12,000
  • Tax paid: $30,000
  • Purchase of equipment: $80,000
  • Sale of equipment (proceeds): $20,000
  • Issue of shares: $50,000
  • Dividends paid: $40,000
  • Opening cash: $20,000

Operating:

  • Operating profit: $150,000
  • Add: Depreciation: $25,000
  • Less: Profit on disposal: ($5,000)
  • Less: Inventory increase: ($10,000)
  • Add: Receivables decrease: $8,000
  • Add: Payables increase: $5,000
  • Less: Interest paid: ($12,000)
  • Less: Tax paid: ($30,000)
  • = $131,000

Investing:

  • Purchase of equipment: ($80,000)
  • Sale proceeds: $20,000
  • = ($60,000)

Financing:

  • Issue of shares: $50,000
  • Dividends paid: ($40,000)
  • = $10,000

Net increase: $131,000 - $60,000 + $10,000 = $81,000

Closing cash: $20,000 + $81,000 = $101,000

⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: Remember the working capital rule: increases in current assets use cash (subtract), decreases release cash (add). Increases in current liabilities provide cash (add), decreases use cash (subtract).

Summary: Chapter 3

  • Operating: start with profit, add back depreciation, adjust for working capital changes
  • Investing: purchase/sale of non-current assets
  • Financing: shares, loans, dividends
  • Working capital: assets inverse (increase = subtract), liabilities direct (increase = add)
  • Closing cash = Opening cash + Net increase

▶ 4. Ratio Analysis

4.1 Purpose of Ratio Analysis

Ratios help users assess:

  • Profitability
  • Liquidity
  • Efficiency
  • Gearing (Financial Structure)
  • Investment Potential

4.2 Profitability Ratios

Gross Profit Margin:
(Gross Profit / Revenue) x 100%

Net Profit Margin:
(Net Profit / Revenue) x 100%

Return on Capital Employed (ROCE):
(Operating Profit / Capital Employed) x 100%
Capital Employed = Equity + Non-Current Liabilities (or Total Assets - Current Liabilities)

Return on Equity (ROE):
(Net Profit / Shareholders' Equity) x 100%
Worked Example

Worked Example: Profitability Ratios

Given:

  • Revenue: $500,000
  • Cost of sales: $300,000
  • Gross profit: $200,000
  • Operating expenses: $120,000
  • Operating profit: $80,000
  • Interest: $10,000
  • Tax: $21,000
  • Net profit: $49,000
  • Capital employed: $400,000
  • Shareholders' equity: $300,000

Calculations:

  • Gross profit margin = ($200,000 / $500,000) x 100% = 40%
  • Net profit margin = ($49,000 / $500,000) x 100% = 9.8%
  • ROCE = ($80,000 / $400,000) x 100% = 20%
  • ROE = ($49,000 / $300,000) x 100% = 16.3%

4.3 Liquidity Ratios

Current Ratio:
Current Assets / Current Liabilities

Acid Test (Quick) Ratio:
(Current Assets - Inventory) / Current Liabilities

Interpretation:
Current ratio > 1: Current assets cover current liabilities
Acid test > 1: Can pay immediate debts without selling inventory

4.4 Efficiency Ratios

Inventory Turnover:
Cost of Sales / Average Inventory
Average Inventory = (Opening + Closing) / 2

Receivables Collection Period:
(Trade Receivables / Credit Sales) x 365 days

Payables Payment Period:
(Trade Payables / Credit Purchases) x 365 days

Asset Turnover:
Revenue / Total Assets

4.5 Gearing Ratios

Debt-to-Equity Ratio:
Non-Current Liabilities / Shareholders' Equity

Gearing Ratio:
Non-Current Liabilities / Capital Employed x 100%

Interest Cover:
Operating Profit / Finance Costs

4.6 Investor Ratios

Earnings Per Share (EPS):
Net Profit / Number of Ordinary Shares

Dividend Per Share (DPS):
Total Ordinary Dividends / Number of Ordinary Shares

Dividend Cover:
Net Profit / Total Dividends
or EPS / DPS

Price-Earnings Ratio (P/E):
Market Price per Share / EPS
⚠ Exam Tip
Exam Tip: When calculating ratios, always use average figures for balance sheet items if opening and closing figures are available. This gives a more representative figure for the period.

Summary: Chapter 4

  • Profitability: Gross margin, net margin, ROCE, ROE
  • Liquidity: Current ratio, acid test
  • Efficiency: Inventory turnover, collection period, payment period, asset turnover
  • Gearing: Debt-to-equity, gearing ratio, interest cover
  • Investment: EPS, DPS, dividend cover, P/E ratio
  • Always compare ratios to previous years or industry benchmarks

The accumulated fund is the net assets (capital) of the organization. It is the equivalent of capital in a sole trader business.

Accumulated Fund:

Subscription Income:
Cash received during year
Add: Subscriptions in arrears (owed but not received)

Less: Subscriptions prepaid this year (not yet earned)
Less: Subscriptions in arrears last year (now received)

Worked Example: Subscriptions

▶ 2. Differentiation

2.1 Basic Rules

Power Rule:
If y = x to the power n, then dy/dx = n x x to the power (n-1)

Constant Rule:
If y = c, then dy/dx = 0

Constant Multiple:
If y = c x f(x), then dy/dx = c x f'(x)

Sum/Difference:
If y = f(x) +/- g(x), then dy/dx = f'(x) +/- g'(x)

2.2 Product Rule

If y = u x v:
dy/dx = u x dv/dx + v x du/dx

2.3 Quotient Rule

If y = u / v:
dy/dx = (v x du/dx - u x dv/dx) / v squared

2.4 Chain Rule

If y = f(g(x)):
dy/dx = f'(g(x)) x g'(x)
Or: dy/dx = dy/du x du/dx

2.5 Derivatives of Exponential and Logarithmic Functions

Exponential:
If y = e to the power x, then dy/dx = e to the power x
If y = e to the power u, then dy/dx = e to the power u x du/dx

Logarithmic:
If y = ln(x), then dy/dx = 1/x
If y = ln(u), then dy/dx = (1/u) x du/dx

2.6 Partial Differentiation

For z = f(x, y):
Partial derivative with respect to x: treat y as constant
Partial derivative with respect to y: treat x as constant

Second-order partials:
fxx, fyy, fxy, fyx
If continuous, fxy = fyx (Clairaut's theorem)

2.7 Applications in Economics

Marginal Functions:
Marginal Cost (MC) = d(TC)/dQ
Marginal Revenue (MR) = d(TR)/dQ
Marginal Profit = d(Profit)/dQ

Profit Maximization:
Profit is maximized when MR = MC
Or when d(Profit)/dQ = 0 and second derivative < 0

Price Elasticity of Demand:
PED = (dQ/dP) x (P/Q)

2.8 Optimization

  • First-order condition: f'(x) = 0 (critical point)
  • Second-order condition: f''(x) < 0 for maximum, f''(x) > 0 for minimum

2.9 Constrained Optimization (Lagrange Multipliers)

To maximize f(x,y) subject to g(x,y) = c:
Form Lagrangian: L = f(x,y) - lambda(g(x,y) - c)
Solve: partial L / partial x = 0
      partial L / partial y = 0
      partial L / partial lambda = 0

▶ 3. Integration

3.1 Basic Integration Rules

Power Rule:
Integral of x to the power n dx = (x to the power (n+1))/(n+1) + C, for n not equal to -1

Constant Multiple:
Integral of k x f(x) dx = k x integral of f(x) dx

Sum/Difference:
Integral of (f +/- g) dx = integral of f dx +/- integral of g dx

Exponential:
Integral of e to the power x dx = e to the power x + C

Logarithmic:
Integral of 1/x dx = ln|x| + C

3.2 Definite Integrals

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:
Integral from a to b of f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a)
where F is the antiderivative of f

3.3 Applications

Area Under Curve:
Area = integral from a to b of f(x) dx

Consumer Surplus:
CS = integral from 0 to Q of demand curve dQ - P x Q

Producer Surplus:
PS = P x Q - integral from 0 to Q of supply curve dQ

Summary: Chapter 3

  • Integration is the reverse of differentiation
  • Power rule: add 1 to exponent, divide by new exponent
  • Definite integral gives area under curve
  • Consumer surplus: value consumers get above price paid
  • Producer surplus: value producers get above cost

▶ 5. Linear Programming

5.1 Formulating LP Problems

  1. Identify decision variables
  2. Write objective function (maximize or minimize)
  3. Write constraints as inequalities
  4. Non-negativity constraints

5.2 Graphical Method

Steps:
1. Plot constraints on graph
2. Identify feasible region (satisfies all constraints)
3. Find corner points of feasible region
4. Evaluate objective function at each corner point
5. Select optimal solution

5.3 Special Cases

  • Multiple optimal solutions: Objective function parallel to constraint
  • Unbounded solution: Feasible region extends infinitely in direction of optimization
  • Infeasible: No feasible region exists

5.4 Simplex Method (Introduction)

  • Algebraic method for solving LP problems
  • Uses slack variables to convert inequalities to equations
  • Iterative process moving from one corner point to better one
  • More efficient than graphical for problems with many variables
Worked Example

Worked Example: Linear Programming

Maximize Profit = 3x + 2y

Subject to:

x + y <= 10

2x + y <= 16

x >= 0, y >= 0

Profit at (0,10) = 20

  • LP: optimize objective subject to constraints
  • Graphical method: plot constraints, find feasible region, check corners

  • Slack variables convert inequalities to equations

▶ 6. Financial Mathematics

6.1 Simple Interest

Simple Interest:
I = P x r x t
A = P + I = P(1 + rt)
P = principal, r = annual rate, t = time in years

6.2 Compound Interest

Compound Interest:
A = P(1 + r/n) to the power (nt)
A = future value, P = principal, r = annual rate
n = compounding periods per year, t = years

Effective Annual Rate (EAR):
EAR = (1 + r/n) to the power n - 1

Continuous Compounding:
A = P x e to the power (rt)

6.3 Annuities

Future Value of Ordinary Annuity:
FV = PMT x (((1 + r) to the power n - 1) / r)

Present Value of Ordinary Annuity:
PV = PMT x ((1 - (1 + r) to the power -n) / r)

Perpetuity:
PV = PMT / r

6.4 Net Present Value (NPV)

NPV Formula:
NPV = sum of (Cash Flow_t / (1 + r) to the power t) - Initial Investment

Decision Rule:
NPV > 0: Accept project
NPV < 0: Reject project
NPV = 0: Indifferent

6.5 Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

IRR:
The discount rate that makes NPV = 0

Decision Rule:
Accept if IRR > required rate of return

Summary: Chapter 6

  • Simple interest: I = Prt
  • Compound interest: A = P(1 + r/n) to the power nt
  • Annuities: series of equal payments. FV and PV formulas
  • Perpetuity: PV = PMT/r
  • NPV > 0: accept. IRR > required rate: accept

▶ 7. Time Series Analysis

7.1 Components of Time Series

  • Trend (T): Long-term direction (upward, downward, stationary)
  • Seasonal (S): Regular patterns repeating within a year
  • Cyclical (C): Fluctuations lasting more than one year (business cycle)
  • Irregular (I): Random, unpredictable variations

7.2 Models

Additive Model:
Y = T + S + C + I

Multiplicative Model:
Y = T x S x C x I

When seasonal variation is constant: use additive
When seasonal variation increases with trend: use multiplicative

7.3 Moving Averages

Centered Moving Average (for even number of seasons):
Calculate 4-period (or 12-period) moving average
Center by averaging consecutive moving averages

Moving average smooths out seasonal and irregular components
Leaving approximately trend + cyclical

7.4 Seasonal Indices

Seasonal Index:
Seasonal Index = (Actual Value / Trend) x 100

Deseasonalized Value:
Additive: Actual - Seasonal
Multiplicative: Actual / Seasonal

Summary: Chapter 7

  • Components: trend, seasonal, cyclical, irregular
  • Additive: Y = T + S + C + I
  • Multiplicative: Y = T x S x C x I
  • Moving averages smooth seasonal and irregular
  • Seasonal indices measure seasonal effect

▶ 8. Index Numbers

8.1 Price Indices

Laspeyres Price Index:
L = (sum(P1 x Q0) / sum(P0 x Q0)) x 100
Uses base period quantities
Tends to overstate inflation (substitution bias)

Paasche Price Index:
P = (sum(P1 x Q1) / sum(P0 x Q1)) x 100
Uses current period quantities
Tends to understate inflation

Fisher Ideal Index:
F = sqrt(Laspeyres x Paasche)
Compromise between Laspeyres and Paasche

8.2 Consumer Price Index (CPI)

  • Measures change in price level of basket of consumer goods
  • Used to measure inflation
  • Used to adjust wages, pensions, contracts

8.3 Deflating

Real Value:
Real Value = (Nominal Value / Price Index) x 100

Converting nominal to real removes effect of inflation

Summary: Chapter 8

  • Laspeyres: base quantities. Paasche: current quantities
  • Fisher: geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche
  • CPI measures consumer price inflation
  • Deflating converts nominal to real values
