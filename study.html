Q1: Under the accrual basis of accounting, revenue is recognised when:
  • A) cash is received from the customer
  • B) the performance obligation is satisfied

  • D) the goods are ordered by the customer

Answer: B
Under AASB 15 / IFRS 15, revenue is recognised when (or as) the entity satisfies a performance obligation by transferring control of goods or services to the customer. This is the core principle of the accrual basis, regardless of when cash is received.

  • D) Income tax expense

Answer: B
Other comprehensive income (OCI) is reported separately in the Statement of Profit or Loss and Other Comprehensive Income, or in a standalone Statement of Other Comprehensive Income. It is not part of the profit or loss section itself.
Q3: A debit entry to the Allowance for Doubtful Debts account most likely indicates:
  • A) an increase in the allowance due to higher expected defaults
  • B) the write-off of a specific uncollectible receivable
  • C) the recovery of a previously written-off debt
  • D) a decrease in credit sales during the period

The Allowance for Doubtful Debts is a contra-asset account with a normal credit balance. A debit to this account reduces the allowance, which typically occurs when a specific receivable is written off against the allowance (Dr Allowance, Cr Accounts Receivable).
Q4: Under the perpetual inventory system, the cost of goods sold is recorded:
  • A) at the end of the accounting period after a physical stocktake
  • B) only when the inventory is purchased
  • C) at the time of each inventory sale
  • D) as an adjusting journal entry at year-end
Show Answer
Answer: C
In a perpetual inventory system, the inventory and cost of goods sold accounts are updated continuously. Every sale triggers a simultaneous entry to record the revenue and the associated cost of goods sold, using inventory records that track quantities and costs in real time.
Q5: Which inventory costing method typically results in the highest ending inventory valuation during a period of rising prices?
  • A) First-In, First-Out (FIFO)
  • B) Last-In, First-Out (LIFO)
  • C) Weighted Average Cost
  • D) Specific Identification
Show Answer
Answer: A
Under FIFO, the oldest (cheaper) costs are assigned to cost of goods sold first, leaving the most recent (higher) costs in ending inventory. In periods of rising prices, this produces a higher ending inventory value compared to weighted average or LIFO. (Note: LIFO is not permitted under AASB 102 / IAS 2.)

Answer: B
AASB 116 requires depreciation to cease when the asset is derecognised or classified as held for sale (or included in a disposal group classified as held for sale) in accordance with AASB 5. Even if fully depreciated, if the residual value is non-zero, the asset is not necessarily derecognised.

Q1: Explain the difference between the cash basis and accrual basis of accounting, and discuss why AASB standards require the accrual basis.Conceptual Framework / Accrual Accounting
Answer
Under the cash basis, transactions are recorded only when cash is received or paid. Revenue is recognised upon cash receipt, and expenses are recognised upon cash payment. This method is simple but can distort financial performance, especially when cash flows and economic activity occur in different periods.

Under the accrual basis, transactions are recorded when they occur, regardless of when cash is exchanged. Revenue is recognised when performance obligations are satisfied (AASB 15), and expenses are recognised when incurred (matched to revenues or allocated systematically).

AASB standards require the accrual basis because it provides a more faithful representation of an entity's financial position and performance. It aligns with the Conceptual Framework's qualitative characteristics of relevance and faithful representation, ensuring that assets, liabilities, income, and expenses reflect the underlying economic substance rather than merely cash movements.

(Alternatively, the discount may be recorded as a reduction of revenue depending on entity policy, but the gross method shown above is common in introductory courses.)
Q3: A company purchases equipment for $50,000 on 1 July 2025. The equipment has an estimated useful life of 5 years and a residual value of $5,000. Calculate depreciation expense for the year ended 30 June 2026 using both the straight-line and diminishing balance (reducing balance) methods at 40%.Non-Current Assets / Depreciation
Answer
Straight-line method:
Depreciable amount = Cost − Residual value = $50,000 − $5,000 = $45,000
Annual depreciation = $45,000 ÷ 5 years = $9,000

Diminishing balance method (40%):
Depreciation = Carrying amount × Rate = $50,000 × 40% = $20,000

Note: under the diminishing balance method, the residual value is not subtracted before applying the rate (unlike straight-line), though the asset cannot be depreciated below its residual value. The carrying amount at 30 June 2026 would be $30,000 ($50,000 − $20,000).
Q4: Describe the purpose and structure of the Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet) in accordance with AASB 101, and explain how it differs from the Statement of Profit or Loss.Financial Reporting / AASB 101
Answer
The Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet) presents an entity's assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time. Its purpose is to provide users with information about the entity's financial position, including its economic resources, claims against those resources, and the net assets (equity) attributable to owners.

Under AASB 101, it must present current and non-current classifications for assets and liabilities unless a liquidity presentation is more relevant. Minimum line items include property, plant and equipment; investment property; intangible assets; financial assets; inventories; trade and other receivables; cash and cash equivalents; trade and other payables; provisions; financial liabilities; tax liabilities; and issued capital and reserves.

The Statement of Profit or Loss (Income Statement) reports financial performance over a period of time, showing revenues, expenses, and profit or loss. While the Balance Sheet is a snapshot of position, the Income Statement is a flow measure of performance. They are linked through retained earnings: profit increases equity, while losses and dividends decrease it.
Q5: On 30 June 2027, after preparing the trial balance, a company's accountant discovers that a $2,000 payment for repairs was incorrectly debited to the Equipment account. Prepare the correcting journal entry and explain how this error would have affected the financial statements if left uncorrected.Errors / Adjusting Entries
Answer
Correcting journal entry:
  Dr Repairs Expense                          $2,000
    Cr Equipment                                               $2,000

Effects if left uncorrected:
• Assets overstated: Equipment (non-current asset) would be $2,000 higher than it should be.
• Expenses understated: Repairs Expense would be $2,000 lower, leading to overstated profit before tax.
• Equity overstated: Because profit is overstated, retained earnings (and therefore total equity) would also be overstated by $2,000.
• Future depreciation overstated: The capitalised $2,000 would be depreciated over the asset's useful life, causing future expenses to be higher than they should be.

This is an error of principle: a revenue expenditure was incorrectly treated as capital expenditure.

Higher discount rates reduce the present value of future cash inflows, which typically lowers NPV. NPV and discount rate are inversely related for conventional projects.

  • B) Sharpe Ratio
  • C) Treynor Ratio
  • D) Beta Coefficient
Show Answer
Answer: B
The Sharpe Ratio calculates excess return over the risk-free rate divided by total risk (standard deviation). The Treynor Ratio uses systematic risk (beta) instead.
Q5: In the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), what does Beta measure?

  • D) The risk-free rate of return

Beta measures an asset's sensitivity to market movements (systematic risk). A beta of 1 means the asset moves with the market; above 1 means more volatile than the market.

Unsystematic (idiosyncratic) risk is firm-specific and can be reduced through diversification. Product recalls, management changes, and labour strikes are examples. The other options are systematic risks affecting the whole market.

Q8: What happens to the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) if a firm increases its debt level, assuming the traditional view of capital structure?

Under the traditional view, moderate debt lowers WACC because debt is cheaper and interest is tax-deductible. Beyond an optimal point, financial distress and agency costs cause WACC to rise.

Q: Explain the concept of the Security Market Line (SML) and how it is used to determine whether a security is fairly priced. CAPM & Asset Pricing
Answer
The Security Market Line (SML) is a graphical representation of the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), plotting expected return against systematic risk (beta). The formula is E(Ri) = Rf + βi(E(Rm) − Rf), where Rf is the risk-free rate, βi is the asset's beta, and E(Rm) is the expected market return. If a security plots above the SML, it is undervalued (offers higher return than required for its risk). If below, it is overvalued. Securities on the line are fairly priced. Investors use the SML to make buy/sell decisions and to evaluate portfolio performance relative to risk-adjusted benchmarks.
Q: Calculate the future value of $5,000 invested today for 8 years at an annual interest rate of 6%, compounded annually. Show your working. Time Value of Money
Answer
Using the future value formula: FV = PV × (1 + r)^n. Here PV = $5,000, r = 0.06, n = 8. FV = 5,000 × (1.06)^8 = 5,000 × 1.593848 = $7,969.24 (rounded to two decimal places). Compounding means interest earns interest over time, so the growth accelerates each year. If compounding were semi-annual, the rate per period would be 3% over 16 periods, yielding a slightly higher FV.
Q: Discuss the trade-off theory of capital structure and explain why firms do not finance entirely with debt despite the tax shield benefit. Capital Structure
Answer
The trade-off theory posits that firms balance the benefits of debt (tax shields from interest deductibility) against the costs (financial distress, bankruptcy risk, and agency costs). As leverage increases, the probability of default rises, leading to higher expected bankruptcy costs, loss of customer/supplier confidence, and restrictive covenants. At low debt levels, the tax shield dominates and firm value rises. Beyond an optimal debt ratio, marginal distress costs exceed marginal tax benefits, causing firm value to decline. Thus, firms target an optimal capital structure rather than 100% debt.
Q: Compare and contrast systematic risk and unsystematic risk. Why can only one of these be priced in equilibrium according to the CAPM? Risk & Return
Answer
Systematic risk (market risk) affects all assets and cannot be eliminated through diversification; examples include interest rate changes, inflation, and geopolitical events. Unsystematic risk (specific risk) is unique to a firm or industry—such as management quality, product recalls, or strikes—and can be reduced or eliminated by holding a well-diversified portfolio. In equilibrium, only systematic risk is priced because rational investors can diversify away unsystematic risk at no cost. Therefore, the market only rewards investors for bearing non-diversifiable risk, which is measured by beta in the CAPM framework.
Q: A company is evaluating two mutually exclusive projects, X and Y. Project X has an IRR of 18% and NPV of $8,000 at a 10% discount rate. Project Y has an IRR of 15% and NPV of $12,000 at the same rate. Which project should the firm accept and why? Capital Budgeting
Answer
The firm should accept Project Y because it has the higher NPV ($12,000 vs $8,000). For mutually exclusive projects, NPV is the superior decision criterion because it measures the absolute increase in shareholder wealth and assumes reinvestment at the required rate of return. IRR can be misleading here: it assumes reinvestment at the IRR itself, and scale differences or timing differences can cause IRR to rank projects differently from NPV. Since the goal is to maximise firm value, the project adding the most dollar value (higher NPV) should be chosen, even if its percentage return (IRR) is lower.

Q: Explain the doctrine of consideration and discuss whether it is always necessary for the enforcement of a promise under Australian contract law. Contract Formation
Answer
Consideration is something of value (a benefit or detriment) exchanged between parties to a contract; it is a fundamental requirement for enforceability of simple contracts. The promisee must provide consideration for the promisor's promise, and past consideration is generally insufficient (Roscorla v Thomas). However, consideration is not always necessary. Promissory estoppel, recognised in Waltons Stores v Maher and developed in Commonwealth v Verwayen, may enforce a promise where the promisee has relied on it to their detriment, even absent consideration. Additionally, deeds are enforceable without consideration because of their formal execution. Recent academic debate questions whether consideration remains a meaningful threshold or an unnecessary formality in modern contract law.
Q: A company director approves a transaction that benefits their spouse's business at the expense of the company. Identify the statutory duties breached and outline the potential consequences under the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth). Corporations Law
Answer
The director likely breaches s 181 (good faith and proper purpose), s 182 (improper use of position), and s 183 (improper use of information) of the Corporations Act. Section 181 requires directors to act in the best interests of the company; s 182 prohibits using their position to gain an advantage for themselves or someone else or to cause detriment to the company. Q: Distinguish between a condition, a warranty, and an intermediate term in a contract. Explain the remedies available for breach of each. Contract Terms
Answer
A condition is an essential term going to the root of the contract; breach allows the innocent party to terminate and claim damages (Associated Newspapers v Bancks). A warranty is a less important, subsidiary term; breach only entitles the innocent party to damages, not termination (Bettini v Gye). An intermediate (or innominate) term does not fit neatly into either category; the remedy depends on the severity of the breach. If the consequences are serious and go to the root of the contract, the innocent party may terminate; otherwise, only damages are available (Hong Kong Fir Shipping v Kawasaki). This approach provides flexibility but introduces uncertainty at the time of contracting.
Q: Discuss the liability of a company for the torts committed by its employees during the course of employment. Refer to the relevant legal principles and case law. Torts & Agency
Answer
A company can be vicariously liable for torts committed by employees acting in the course of employment under the doctrine of respondereat superior (let the employer answer). The key test is whether the employee's conduct was authorised or a wrongful mode of performing an authorised act (Bugge v Brown; Deatons v Flew). The 'close connection' test from Lister v Hesley Hall (UK) and adopted in Australia determines whether the tort was so closely connected to employment that it is fair to impose liability. Companies may also be directly liable for negligence in hiring, training, or supervising employees. Defences include that the employee was on a 'frolic of their own' (acting outside the scope of employment), which breaks the chain of vicarious liability.
Q: Explain the concept of insolvent trading under s 588G of the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth). What defences are available to a director accused of allowing a company to trade while insolvent? Corporate Insolvency
Answer
Section 588G imposes a duty on directors to prevent a company from incurring debts if there are reasonable grounds to suspect insolvency. A director breaches the provision if: (1) they are a director at the time the debt is incurred, (2) the company is insolvent or becomes insolvent by incurring the debt, and (3) there are reasonable grounds for suspecting insolvency. Defences under s 588H include: (a) the director had reasonable grounds to expect, and did expect, that the company was solvent (often relying on financial information); (b) the director reasonably relied on information from a competent and reliable person indicating solvency; (c) the director took all reasonable steps to prevent the debt from being incurred; or (d) due to illness, the director did not take part in management at that time. The safe harbour provision (s 588GA) may also protect directors who are pursuing a course of action reasonably likely to lead to a better outcome for the company than immediate administration or liquidation.

Q: Explain the law of diminishing marginal returns and illustrate with a numerical example.Production Theory
Answer
The law of diminishing marginal returns states that as successive units of a variable input (e.g., labour) are added to fixed inputs (e.g., capital and land), there is a point beyond which the additional output (marginal product) from each extra unit of the variable input begins to decline.

Example: Consider a factory with 5 machines (fixed).
• 1 worker produces 10 units/day (MP = 10)
• 2 workers produce 25 units/day (MP = 15)
• 3 workers produce 36 units/day (MP = 11)
• 4 workers produce 40 units/day (MP = 4)
• 5 workers produce 42 units/day (MP = 2)

After the 2nd worker, each additional worker contributes less to total output than the previous one. This occurs because fixed inputs become overcrowded or overused. The law is central to understanding short-run cost curves and explains why marginal cost eventually rises.
Q: Distinguish between GDP and GNP. Which measure is more commonly used for domestic policy analysis and why?Macroeconomic Measurement

GDP is more commonly used for domestic policy analysis because it directly reflects economic activity occurring within the country, making it more relevant for assessing domestic employment, tax bases, and infrastructure needs. It is also easier to measure accurately using domestic data collection systems.
Q: Using a diagram, explain how a price ceiling set below the equilibrium price affects market outcomes. Discuss one real-world example.Market Intervention
Answer
A price ceiling is a legal maximum price set by the government. When set below the equilibrium price (a binding price ceiling), it creates a shortage because quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at that price.

Diagram explanation:
• Draw standard supply (upward sloping) and demand (downward sloping) curves intersecting at equilibrium price P* and quantity Q*.
• Draw a horizontal price ceiling line at Pc below P*.
• At Pc, quantity demanded (Qd) is read off the demand curve; quantity supplied (Qs) is read off the supply curve.
• Since Qd > Qs, a shortage of (Qd - Qs) occurs.

Consequences include:
1. Shortages and queuing
2. Black markets where goods trade above the legal maximum
3. Reduced quality as producers cut costs
4. Non-price rationing mechanisms (e.g., waiting lists, favouritism)

Real-world example: Rent control in cities like New York or Melbourne (historically) keeps rental prices below market rates, leading to housing shortages, reduced maintenance incentives for landlords, and long waiting lists for affordable housing.

Q: Explain the functions of money and discuss why cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin may struggle to fulfil one or more of these functions effectively.Monetary Economics
Answer
Money serves three primary functions:

1. Medium of exchange: Money eliminates the need for barter by acting as an intermediary accepted in transactions. It reduces transaction costs and the problem of double coincidence of wants.

2. Unit of account: Money provides a common measure of value, allowing prices to be quoted and economic value to be compared. It simplifies accounting and economic calculation.

3. Store of value: Money allows purchasing power to be transferred from the present to the future. It retains value over time so people can save and plan.

Why Bitcoin struggles:
• Medium of exchange: Limited merchant acceptance, high transaction fees during network congestion, and slow confirmation times make it impractical for everyday purchases compared to fiat currency or credit cards.
• Unit of account: Extreme price volatility means prices denominated in Bitcoin fluctuate wildly, making it unreliable for long-term contracts or price setting. Most goods are still priced in national currencies.
• Store of value: While some view Bitcoin as "digital gold," its volatility makes it risky as a store of value. A 20-30% price swing in a week is common, unlike stable fiat currencies which typically fluctuate by small percentages annually.

These limitations explain why central banks continue to focus on fiat currency and are exploring more stable central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) rather than adopting decentralised cryptocurrencies as primary money.

Q: Explain the difference between Type I and Type II errors in hypothesis testing, and describe a business scenario where each would be costly.Hypothesis Testing
Answer
Type I Error (False Positive): Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true. The probability of a Type I error is denoted by α (significance level).

Type II Error (False Negative): Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is actually false. The probability of a Type II error is denoted by β. The power of a test is 1 - β.

Business scenario — Pharmaceutical company testing a new drug:
• Type I Error (costly): The company concludes the drug is effective when it actually is not. They invest millions in production, marketing, and distribution. When the drug fails to help patients, the company faces lawsuits, regulatory penalties, and reputational damage.

• Type II Error (costly): The company concludes the drug is ineffective when it actually works. They abandon a potentially life-saving treatment, losing future revenue and failing to help patients who could have benefited. A competitor might later develop and patent a similar drug.

In practice, businesses must balance these risks. Lowering α reduces Type I errors but increases Type II errors (for fixed sample size), so the choice of significance level should reflect the relative costs of each error type.

Q3: Explain why absorption costing and variable costing may produce different profit figures for the same period, and identify the condition under which both methods report the same profit.Absorption vs Variable Costing
Answer
The difference arises from the treatment of fixed manufacturing overhead. Under absorption costing, fixed manufacturing overhead is treated as a product cost and included in inventory; it is expensed only when the inventory is sold. Under variable costing, fixed manufacturing overhead is treated as a period cost and expensed in full in the period incurred.

When production exceeds sales, absorption costing defers some fixed overhead in ending inventory, resulting in higher profit than variable costing. When sales exceed production (inventory decreases), previously deferred fixed overhead is released, causing absorption costing profit to be lower.

Both methods report the same profit when production equals sales (i.e., there is no change in inventory levels over the period). In this case, no fixed overhead is deferred or released from inventory.

Q2: Explain the consequences of omitted variable bias in multiple regression. Under what conditions does it arise, and how can it be addressed?Econometric Theory
Answer
Omitted variable bias (OVB) occurs when a relevant variable correlated with both the dependent variable and an included regressor is excluded from the model. The bias in the estimated coefficient is:

Bias(β̂₁) = β₂ · δ₁

where β₂ is the effect of the omitted variable on Y, and δ₁ is the coefficient from regressing the omitted variable on the included regressor. OVB requires two conditions: (1) the omitted variable affects Y (β₂ ≠ 0), and (2) the omitted variable is correlated with an included regressor (δ₁ ≠ 0).
