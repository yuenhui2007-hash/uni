3. Reasons for Regular Bank Reconciliation

  • Fraud detection: Regular reconciliation helps identify unauthorized transactions, forged cheques, or embezzlement early. Discrepancies between the cash book and bank statement may indicate fraudulent activity that requires investigation.

Model Answer

1. Key Features

(a) Sole Trader: A business owned and operated by one person. The owner has unlimited liability — personal assets can be seized to pay business debts. The owner retains all profits but bears all losses. There is no separate legal identity from the owner. Setup is simple with minimal legal formalities and regulatory requirements. The owner makes all decisions independently but may face constraints in raising capital.

(b) Private Limited Company: A separate legal entity owned by shareholders. Liability is limited to the amount invested in shares. The company can enter contracts, own property, and sue/be sued in its own name. Ownership is divided into shares that are not publicly traded. It must comply with the Corporations Act requirements: registration with ASIC, annual reports, audited financial statements (if thresholds met), and a company constitution. Profits are distributed as dividends after tax.

Caveat: The owner should budget for compliance costs (~$3,000–5,000 annually) and consider engaging a company secretary or accountant to manage regulatory obligations.

Model Answer

1. Definitions

Penetration Pricing: Setting a low initial price to rapidly gain market share. The price is deliberately set below competitors to attract price-sensitive customers, discourage new entrants, and achieve economies of scale through high volume. Best suited for: mass-market products, highly competitive markets, products with low differentiation, and markets with price-elastic demand.

Price Skimming: Setting a high initial price targeting early adopters and customers with low price sensitivity, then gradually lowering the price over time. Captures consumer surplus from different market segments sequentially. Best suited for: innovative products with patent protection, strong brand loyalty, inelastic demand, and significant R&D investment to recover.

2. Diagram Analysis

Penetration Pricing over Product Life Cycle:

  • Introduction: Low price → high volume, revenue grows rapidly
  • Growth: Maintain low price to consolidate market share, revenue peaks as volume maximizes
  • Maturity: Slight price increases possible as market dominance established, revenue stable
  • Decline: Lower prices further to clear inventory, revenue falls

Price Skimming over Product Life Cycle:

  • Introduction: High price → low volume but high margin per unit, revenue starts lower but margin is high
  • Growth: Gradual price reduction captures next customer segment, revenue accelerates
  • Maturity: Price approaches market average, volume maximizes, revenue peaks later than penetration
  • Decline: Low prices to clear stock, revenue declines

A fast-fashion retailer has been criticised for environmental damage, worker exploitation in developing countries, and misleading "green" marketing claims. The board is under pressure from consumers, investors, and regulators to improve its corporate social responsibility (CSR) practices.

Required:

  2. Analyse why CSR has become increasingly important for businesses, considering stakeholder theory and the business case for CSR.
  3. Evaluate three specific CSR initiatives the fast-fashion retailer could implement to address the criticisms, assessing the likely costs and benefits of each.
Model Answer

1. CSR and Triple Bottom Line

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR): A business approach that integrates social and environmental concerns into commercial operations and stakeholder interactions. It extends beyond legal compliance to voluntary actions that benefit society. CSR reflects the recognition that businesses operate within a broader social system and have obligations to multiple stakeholders, not just shareholders.

Triple Bottom Line (TBL): A framework measuring organisational performance across three dimensions:

  • Profit (Economic): Traditional financial performance — revenue, costs, profitability, shareholder returns. This remains essential for business survival and growth.
  • People (Social): Impact on employees, customers, suppliers, and communities. Includes fair wages, safe working conditions, diversity, community engagement, and supply chain ethics.
  • Planet (Environmental): Environmental footprint — carbon emissions, waste generation, resource consumption, pollution, and biodiversity impact.

The TBL argues that sustainable success requires balancing all three dimensions; focusing solely on profit while neglecting people and planet creates long-term risks including reputational damage, regulatory penalties, and resource scarcity.

2. Importance of CSR

Stakeholder Theory (Freeman, 1984): Businesses have responsibilities to all stakeholders who affect or are affected by the organisation's actions — employees, customers, suppliers, communities, environment, and shareholders. Ignoring any stakeholder group creates risk:

  • Customers may boycott products (revenue loss)
  • Employees may leave or unionise (increased costs, disruption)
  • Regulators may impose fines or restrictions (profitability impact)
  • Investors may divest (share price decline, capital access constraints)

Business Case for CSR:

  • Risk management: Proactive CSR reduces regulatory, legal, and reputational risks. Prevention is cheaper than crisis management.
  • Brand differentiation: In competitive markets, CSR credentials attract ethically conscious consumers willing to pay premiums.
  • Talent attraction and retention: Employees increasingly prefer employers with strong ethical values, reducing recruitment and training costs.
  • Investor appeal: ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) investing is growing; companies with strong CSR attract institutional capital.
  • Operational efficiency: Environmental initiatives (energy reduction, waste minimisation) often reduce costs.

Variable cost per unit = Direct materials + Direct labour + Variable MOH + Variable S&A
= $18 + $12 + $6 + $4 = $40

= $55 - $40 = $15 per unit

Contribution margin ratio = Contribution margin / Selling price
= $15 / $55 = 27.27%

2. Break-Even Point

Total fixed costs = Fixed MOH + Fixed S&A = $40,000 + $20,000 = $60,000

Break-even (units) = Total fixed costs / Contribution per unit
= $60,000 / $15 = 4,000 units

Break-even ($) = Total fixed costs / CM ratio
= $60,000 / 0.2727 = $220,000
(Or: 4,000 units x $55 = $220,000)

3. Margin of Safety

Margin of safety (units) = Current sales - Break-even sales
= 3,000 - 4,000 = -1,000 units

Margin of safety (%) = (Current sales - BE sales) / Current sales x 100%
= (3,000 - 4,000) / 3,000 x 100% = -33.3%

Interpretation: Omega is currently operating below break-even. At 3,000 units, the business makes a loss of $15,000 per month (3,000 x $15 - $60,000 = -$15,000). This is a critical finding — the current business model is unsustainable without cost reduction, price increase, or volume growth.

Tom, who had already purchased specialised veneer worth $800 in preparation, receives the revocation email after he had already sent his acceptance by registered post.

Required:

  1. Define and explain the elements required for a valid contract under common law.
  2. Analyse whether a valid contract was formed between Sarah and Tom, applying the postal acceptance rule and the principle of revocation of offers.
  3. Assuming no contract was formed, advise Tom on whether he has any alternative legal remedies and the likelihood of success.
Model Answer

1. Elements of a Valid Contract

A valid contract requires five essential elements under common law:

  • Offer: A clear expression of willingness to be bound on specific terms. Must be distinguished from an invitation to treat (e.g., shop displays, advertisements). In this case, Sarah's email specifying price ($4,500), subject matter (restoration), and time limit (14 days) constitutes a valid offer.
  • Acceptance: Unconditional agreement to the offer's terms. Must be communicated to the offeror. Silence is not acceptance (Felthouse v Bindley, 1862). The acceptance must mirror the offer — counter-offers destroy the original offer (Hyde v Wrench, 1840).
  • Consideration: Something of value exchanged between parties. Must be sufficient (have some value in law) but need not be adequate (fair). Past consideration is generally not valid (Roscorla v Thomas, 1842).
  • Intention to Create Legal Relations: In commercial contexts, this is presumed (Esso Petroleum v Commissioners of Customs and Excise, 1976). Social/domestic agreements are presumed not to have this intention (Balfour v Balfour, 1919).
  • Capacity: Parties must have legal capacity — minors, persons with mental incapacity, and intoxicated persons may lack full capacity.

2. Was a Valid Contract Formed?

Revocation of Offers: An offer can be revoked at any time before acceptance (Payne v Cave, 1789). However, revocation must be communicated to the offeree before acceptance occurs. Revocation by a third party is valid if the offeree learns of it through a reliable source (Dickinson v Dodds, 1876). In this case, Sarah's revocation email was sent but received after Tom's acceptance was posted.

Postal Acceptance Rule (Adams v Lindsell, 1818): Acceptance by post is effective when the letter is properly posted, not when received. Requirements:

  • Post must be a reasonable method of communication (it is, given the 14-day timeframe)
  • Letter must be properly addressed and stamped (registered post satisfies this)
  • Rule can be excluded by the offeror (Sarah did not specify otherwise)

Application: Tom's acceptance was effective the moment he posted it by registered post. Sarah's revocation, received afterwards, was ineffective. A valid contract was formed for $4,500.

3. Alternative Remedies (if no contract)

If no contract existed, Tom could explore:

  • Promissory Estoppel (Central London Property Trust v High Trees House, 1947): If Sarah made a clear promise that Tom relied upon to his detriment (purchasing $800 veneer), and it would be inequitable to allow Sarah to go back on her promise, the court may enforce the promise. However, estoppel is a shield not a sword — it suspends rights rather than creating new ones.
  • Negligent Misstatement: Unlikely unless Sarah made a false statement carelessly that induced Tom's reliance.
  • Restitution: If Tom conferred a benefit on Sarah, he could claim quantum meruit. Not applicable here as no work was performed.

Conclusion: Tom has a strong case that a contract was formed under the postal acceptance rule. He can sue for specific performance (unique antique) or damages if Sarah refuses to perform.

Model Answer

1. Proving Negligence — Caparo Three-Part Test

Negligence requires: (1) duty of care, (2) breach of duty, and (3) causation and damage.

Duty of Care (Caparo Industries v Dickman, 1990):

  • Foreseeability: The defendant must be able to reasonably foresee that their act or omission would cause harm to the claimant. A supermarket should foresee that unattended spillages cause slips — this is well-established.
  • Proximity: There must be a sufficiently close relationship between the parties. A customer lawfully on premises has a direct relationship with the occupier.
  • Fair, Just and Reasonable: It must be fair to impose a duty. Imposing a duty on businesses to maintain safe premises for customers is fair and reasonable.

Breach of Duty: The defendant must fall below the standard of the reasonable person (objective test, Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks, 1856). Factors:

  • Likelihood of harm (high — spills commonly cause falls)
  • Severity of potential harm (high — fractures are serious)
  • Cost of precautions (low — staff patrols, warning signs cost little)
  • Social utility of the defendant's activity (relevant but not determinative)

Causation and Damage:

  • Factual causation ("but for" test): But for the spillage, the customer would not have fallen.
  • Legal causation (remoteness): A fractured wrist is a foreseeable consequence of a fall — not too remote (The Wagon Mound, 1961).

2. Effectiveness of the Exclusion Clause

Under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL), specifically s.64:

  • Exclusion clauses attempting to exclude liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence in the supply of services are void.
  • Under s.18 ACL, misleading or deceptive conduct (presenting an unenforceable clause as effective) may itself be actionable.

Even at common law, for an exclusion clause to be effective:

  • Incorporation: Must be brought to the customer's attention before or at the time of contract formation. A receipt given after purchase is too late (Olley v Marlborough Court, 1949).
  • Construction: Ambiguous clauses are construed contra proferentem (against the party seeking to rely on them). "Howsoever caused" is broad but cannot exclude liability for negligence where statute prohibits it.
  • Unfair Contract Terms: Under ACL s.23, terms that are unfair (cause significant imbalance, not reasonably necessary, cause detriment) are void. A blanket exclusion of all liability for personal injury is almost certainly unfair.

3. Advice to the Customer

David Ricardo's principle states that countries should specialise in producing goods in which they have a lower opportunity cost, even if one country is absolutely less efficient in all goods. The basis for trade is relative efficiency, not absolute efficiency.

3. Effect of Risk Aversion

A risk-averse decision maker has a concave utility function — each additional dollar provides less utility than the previous one.

  • Large scale under strong demand: $800,000 profit — high utility, but diminishing returns mean the marginal utility of dollars above $500,000 is low.
  • Large scale under weak demand: -$300,000 loss — significant utility loss. The disutility of a $300k loss exceeds the utility gain from a $300k gain (loss aversion, Kahneman & Tversky).
  • Small scale: More certain outcomes ($300k or $50k) — lower variance, higher expected utility for a risk-averse decision maker even if EMV is lower.

Possible outcome: A risk-averse manager might choose the small-scale launch even though EMV is lower ($200,000 vs $360,000), because the certainty-equivalent of the large-scale gamble is reduced by the severe disutility of the potential $300,000 loss. The manager would require a risk premium to accept the large-scale option.

The survey might become more attractive to a risk-averse manager if it reduces the probability of the worst outcome — not for EMV reasons, but for utility-of-certainty reasons.

4. Ethical Implications

  • Algorithmic bias: If training data underrepresents certain demographics (elderly, minorities, rural patients), the model may systematically misclassify these groups, denying them interventions.
  • Privacy: Predictive models require extensive patient data. Consent, data security, and secondary use restrictions must be addressed.
  • Autonomy: Patients flagged as "high risk" may face restricted choices (mandatory home visits, insurance implications) without their input.
  • Resource allocation justice: Targeting resources based on predicted risk may divert care from patients who don't fit the model's patterns but have genuine needs. Predictive models optimise efficiency; ethics also requires equity.
  • Transparency and explainability: Black-box models that cannot explain why a patient is flagged create accountability gaps. Clinicians and patients deserve interpretable reasoning.
  • Feedback loops: If high-risk classification leads to more intensive monitoring, and intensive monitoring detects more problems, the model's predictions become self-fulfilling, reinforcing existing patterns.
