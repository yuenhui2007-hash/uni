ACW2220 — Management Accounting
ACW2220 · 5 Questions
Question 1
Theory
Define management accounting and explain two key differences between management accounting and financial accounting.
Model Answer
Definition: Management accounting is the process of identifying, measuring, analysing, and communicating financial information to managers for planning, controlling, and decision-making within an organisation.

Key Differences:
1. Users: Management accounting serves internal managers; financial accounting serves external stakeholders (investors, regulators, creditors).
2. Regulation: Financial accounting must comply with accounting standards (IFRS/AASB); management accounting has no mandatory standards — formats are tailored to managerial needs.
3. Time focus: Financial accounting is historical; management accounting is forward-looking (budgets, forecasts).
Question 2
Theory
A company produces 5,000 units with total fixed costs of $20,000 and variable costs of $8 per unit. Calculate the total cost and cost per unit at production levels of 4,000 and 6,000 units.
Model Answer
At 4,000 units:
Variable cost = 4,000 × $8 = $32,000
Total cost = $32,000 + $20,000 = $52,000
Cost per unit = $52,000 ÷ 4,000 = $13.00

At 5,000 units (original):
Variable cost = 5,000 × $8 = $40,000
Total cost = $40,000 + $20,000 = $60,000
Cost per unit = $60,000 ÷ 5,000 = $12.00

At 6,000 units:
Variable cost = 6,000 × $8 = $48,000
Total cost = $48,000 + $20,000 = $68,000
Cost per unit = $68,000 ÷ 6,000 = $11.33

Insight: As volume increases, fixed cost is spread over more units, reducing the per-unit cost (economies of scale).
Question 3
Theory
Explain the difference between direct costs and indirect costs, providing two examples of each for a furniture manufacturing business.
Model Answer
Direct Costs: Costs that can be specifically and conveniently traced to a particular cost object (product, department, or project).
Examples for furniture manufacturing:
1. Timber and upholstery materials used in a specific sofa
2. Wages of carpenters working directly on the furniture

Indirect Costs (Overheads): Costs that cannot be easily traced to a specific cost object and must be allocated.
Examples:
1. Factory rent and utilities
2. Depreciation of factory equipment

Why it matters: Accurate classification ensures proper product costing and pricing decisions. Misclassification distorts profitability analysis.
Question 4
Theory
A product sells for $50 per unit, variable cost is $30 per unit, and total fixed costs are $10,000. Calculate the break-even point in units and in sales dollars.
Model Answer
Contribution Margin per unit:
Selling price − Variable cost = $50 − $30 = $20

Break-even in units:
Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin per unit
= $10,000 ÷ $20 = 500 units

Break-even in sales dollars:
500 units × $50 = $25,000

Alternatively: Fixed costs ÷ CM ratio
CM ratio = $20 ÷ $50 = 40%
$10,000 ÷ 0.40 = $25,000

Interpretation: The company must sell 500 units (or $25,000 worth) to cover all costs. Every unit sold beyond 500 contributes $20 to profit.
Question 5
Theory
Describe the three main purposes of budgeting and explain how a flexible budget differs from a static budget.
Model Answer
Three Main Purposes of Budgeting:
1. Planning: Forces managers to think ahead, set targets, and allocate resources effectively.
2. Control: Provides a benchmark against which actual performance is compared; variances highlight areas needing attention.
3. Coordination & Communication: Aligns different departments toward common organisational goals.

Static vs Flexible Budget:
Static budget: Prepared for a single, planned level of activity. Does not change even if actual output differs. Good for planning but poor for control when volumes vary.

Flexible budget: Adjusts costs and revenues to the actual level of activity achieved. Shows what costs should have been at the actual output level. Enables meaningful variance analysis by separating volume effects from efficiency effects.

Question 1
Theory
Explain Porter's Five Forces model and identify one force that is typically strongest for a new airline entering the Australian market.
Model Answer
Porter's Five Forces: A framework for analysing industry competition and profitability.

1. Threat of New Entrants: Barriers to entry (capital, regulations, brand loyalty) protect incumbents.
2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers: Few suppliers (e.g., aircraft manufacturers) can raise prices.
3. Bargaining Power of Buyers: Customers can switch easily if alternatives exist.
4. Threat of Substitutes: Trains, video conferencing, or driving can replace air travel.
5. Competitive Rivalry: Intensity of competition among existing firms.

Strongest force for a new airline in Australia:
Competitive Rivalry — Qantas and Virgin Australia dominate; price wars are intense; customer loyalty programs create switching costs. A new entrant faces brutal competition for market share.

What is a SWOT analysis? Apply it to Netflix, identifying one strength, one weakness, one opportunity, and one threat.

SWOT Analysis: A strategic planning tool that evaluates internal Strengths and Weaknesses, and external Opportunities and Threats.

Netflix SWOT:

Strength: Strong original content library and global brand recognition create customer loyalty and reduce churn.

Weakness: Heavy reliance on debt financing for content production; rising interest rates increase borrowing costs.

Opportunity: Expansion into gaming and live events (sports, concerts) to diversify revenue beyond subscription streaming.

Threat: Intensifying competition from Disney+, Amazon Prime, Apple TV+, and local platforms fragmenting market share and driving up content acquisition costs.

Question 4
Theory
Distinguish between corporate-level strategy and business-level strategy, giving one example of each.
Model Answer
Corporate-Level Strategy: Concerns the overall scope of the organisation and how value is created across multiple business units. It answers: What businesses should we be in?

Example: Alphabet (Google's parent) pursues a related diversification strategy — it operates in search (Google), cloud (Google Cloud), autonomous vehicles (Waymo), and life sciences (Verily), leveraging shared data and AI capabilities across units.

Business-Level Strategy: Concerns how a specific business unit competes within its industry. It answers: How should we compete?

Example: Within Alphabet, Google Search pursues a cost leadership strategy for its advertising platform — massive scale, automated auctions, and low marginal cost per ad impression make it the dominant player. Meanwhile, Waymo pursues differentiation through technological leadership in autonomous driving.

Key distinction: Corporate strategy is about portfolio composition; business strategy is about competitive positioning within a market.
Question 5
Theory
Explain the concept of the Triple Bottom Line (TBL) and why it matters for modern business strategy.
Model Answer
Triple Bottom Line (TBL): A sustainability framework coined by John Elkington that argues companies should measure success not just by profit (the traditional bottom line), but across three dimensions:

1. People (Social): Fair treatment of employees, community impact, ethical supply chains, diversity and inclusion.
2. Planet (Environmental): Carbon footprint, waste reduction, sustainable sourcing, circular economy practices.
3. Profit (Economic): Financial viability and shareholder returns — the traditional measure.

Why it matters:
• Investor pressure: ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) funds now manage trillions of dollars; poor ESG performance can limit capital access.
• Consumer preference: Studies show consumers (especially Gen Z) prefer brands with authentic sustainability commitments.
• Regulatory risk: Carbon pricing, emissions disclosure mandates, and supply chain due diligence laws are expanding globally.
• Long-term resilience: Resource-efficient operations and strong stakeholder relationships reduce operational and reputational risks.

Criticism: TBL can be vague and hard to quantify; "greenwashing" undermines credibility when companies claim sustainability without substance.

BTW1042 — Business Technology
BTW1042 · 5 Questions
Question 1
Theory
Define cloud computing and explain the three main service models: IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS.
Model Answer
Cloud Computing: Delivery of computing services (servers, storage, databases, networking, software) over the internet ("the cloud") on a pay-as-you-go basis. It replaces capital-intensive on-premises infrastructure with scalable, flexible, and cost-efficient resources.

Three Service Models:

1. IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service): Provides virtualised computing resources — servers, storage, networks. The user manages the OS, applications, and data. Example: Amazon EC2, Microsoft Azure VMs. Highest flexibility, highest management burden.

2. PaaS (Platform as a Service): Provides a development platform with tools, libraries, and middleware. The user writes code and manages data; the provider manages the infrastructure and runtime. Example: Google App Engine, Heroku. Balances control with convenience.

3. SaaS (Software as a Service): Delivers fully functional applications over the internet. The user simply uses the software; the provider manages everything. Example: Microsoft 365, Salesforce, Zoom. Lowest control, lowest management burden.
Question 2
Theory
What is a database management system (DBMS)? Contrast SQL and NoSQL databases with one example of each.
Model Answer
DBMS: Software that enables users to create, manage, and manipulate databases. It provides tools for data storage, retrieval, security, integrity, and concurrency control. Acts as an intermediary between users/applications and the physical database.

SQL (Relational) Databases:
Store data in structured tables with predefined schemas. Use SQL (Structured Query Language) for queries. Best for complex transactions and relationships. Example: PostgreSQL — an open-source RDBMS used by enterprises for financial data, CRM systems, and complex reporting.

NoSQL (Non-relational) Databases:
Store data in flexible formats (documents, key-value pairs, graphs, wide-columns). Schema is dynamic. Best for unstructured/big data, rapid scaling, and agile development. Example: MongoDB — a document-oriented database storing JSON-like documents. Used by companies like Uber and eBay for real-time analytics and content management.

When to choose: SQL for structured data with complex relationships; NoSQL for massive scale, unstructured data, or rapid iteration.
Question 3
Theory
Explain the concept of cybersecurity and describe three common types of cyber threats facing businesses today.
Model Answer
Cybersecurity: The practice of protecting systems, networks, programs, devices, and data from digital attacks, unauthorised access, damage, or theft. It encompasses technologies, processes, and controls designed to safeguard confidentiality, integrity, and availability (the CIA triad).

Three Common Threats:

1. Phishing: Fraudulent emails or messages that trick recipients into revealing credentials or downloading malware. Highly effective because it exploits human psychology (urgency, authority). Mitigation: email filtering, user training, multi-factor authentication (MFA).

2. Ransomware: Malware that encrypts a victim's data and demands payment for decryption. Can paralyse operations. High-profile cases: Colonial Pipeline (2021), Optus (2022). Mitigation: regular backups, network segmentation, endpoint detection.

3. SQL Injection: Attackers insert malicious SQL code into input fields to manipulate database queries. Can expose sensitive data, modify records, or delete databases. Mitigation: parameterized queries, input validation, web application firewalls (WAFs).

Business impact: Average cost of a data breach in Australia exceeds $4 million (IBM, 2023). Reputational damage and regulatory fines (under the Privacy Act and GDPR) compound the financial loss.

What is an ERP system? Describe two benefits and two challenges of implementing ERP in a mid-sized company.
Model Answer
ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning): Integrated software suite that manages core business processes — finance, HR, manufacturing, supply chain, CRM, inventory — in a single unified database. Examples: SAP, Oracle NetSuite, Microsoft Dynamics.

Two Benefits:

1. Data integration & visibility: Eliminates data silos. A sales order automatically updates inventory, triggers procurement if stock is low, and records revenue in finance. Real-time dashboards give managers a single source of truth.

2. Process standardisation: Enforces consistent workflows across departments and locations. Reduces errors, improves compliance, and enables scalable growth.

Two Challenges:

1. High cost & complexity: Implementation can cost millions and take 12–24 months. Customisation, data migration, and change management are resource-intensive. Mid-sized companies may lack the budget and IT expertise.

2. Organisational resistance: Employees accustomed to legacy systems may resist new workflows. Without strong leadership support and training, adoption suffers and ROI is delayed. "The software is only as good as the people using it."

2. Ethical considerations: Bias in training data, lack of transparency ("black box" problem), and job displacement are significant concerns requiring governance frameworks.

ECM1953 — Economics
ECM1953 · 5 Questions
Question 1
Theory
Define scarcity and explain why it is the fundamental economic problem that all societies face.
Model Answer
Scarcity: The condition where human wants for goods, services, and resources exceed what is available. Resources (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) are finite, but human desires are effectively infinite.

Why it is the fundamental economic problem:

Because scarcity forces every society to make choices. We cannot have everything we want, so we must decide:
• What to produce? Should we build hospitals or highways?
• How to produce? Labour-intensive or capital-intensive methods?
• For whom to produce? Who gets the goods — the rich, the poor, or everyone equally?

These three questions form the core of economic systems. Different societies answer them differently: market economies use prices and incentives; command economies use central planning; mixed economies combine both.

Key insight: Economics is not the study of money — it is the study of how individuals, businesses, and societies allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants.
Question 2
Theory
Distinguish between a change in demand and a change in quantity demanded, using a relevant example.
Model Answer
Change in Quantity Demanded: A movement along the demand curve caused solely by a change in the good's own price. All other factors held constant (ceteris paribus).

Example: If the price of coffee rises from $4 to $5 per cup, consumers buy less coffee. This is a movement upward along the demand curve — not a shift of the curve.

Change in Demand: A shift of the entire demand curve caused by non-price determinants: income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations, or number of buyers.

Example: A health study reveals coffee reduces heart disease risk. Consumer tastes shift favourably toward coffee. At every price, more coffee is demanded. The demand curve shifts rightward. Even at $4 (the original price), quantity demanded is now higher.

Visual distinction:
• Quantity demanded: point moves along the curve
• Demand: the entire curve moves left or right

This distinction is critical for businesses forecasting sales and policymakers predicting tax revenue.
Question 3
Theory
Explain price elasticity of demand (PED) and calculate PED when price falls from $10 to $8 and quantity demanded rises from 100 to 140 units.
Model Answer
Price Elasticity of Demand (PED): Measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price. It shows how much consumers adjust their purchases when price changes.

Formula: PED = (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price)

Using the midpoint (arc elasticity) method for accuracy:

%ΔQ = (140 − 100) ÷ [(140 + 100) ÷ 2] × 100
= 40 ÷ 120 × 100 = 33.33%

%ΔP = ($8 − $10) ÷ [($8 + $10) ÷ 2] × 100
= (−$2) ÷ $9 × 100 = −22.22%

PED = 33.33% ÷ (−22.22%) = −1.5

(We typically use the absolute value: PED = 1.5)

Interpretation: Since |PED| > 1, demand is elastic. A 1% price decrease leads to a 1.5% increase in quantity demanded. Total revenue rises when price falls (because the % gain in quantity outweighs the % loss in price).

Business implication: The firm should lower price to increase total revenue.
Question 4
Theory
Describe the four phases of the business cycle and identify which phase Australia was likely in during 2023.
Model Answer
The Business Cycle: Fluctuations in economic activity (real GDP) around its long-term growth trend. Four phases:

1. Expansion: GDP grows, unemployment falls, consumer confidence rises, investment increases. Inflation may begin to build. The economy operates above potential.

2. Peak: The highest point of economic activity before contraction begins. Resources are fully employed; inflationary pressures are strongest. The turning point.

3. Contraction (Recession): GDP declines for two consecutive quarters. Unemployment rises, business investment falls, consumer spending drops. If severe and prolonged, becomes a depression.

4. Trough: The lowest point of economic activity. High unemployment, low output, weak confidence. The turning point before recovery begins.

Australia in 2023:
Australia was in the late expansion / approaching peak phase. GDP growth was positive but slowing (1.5–2%). Unemployment was near 50-year lows (~3.5%). However, the RBA was aggressively raising interest rates (from 0.1% to 4.35%) to combat inflation (peaked at ~7.8%). This monetary tightening was designed to cool the economy and avoid overheating — a classic late-expansion policy response.

By late 2023, many economists expected Australia to enter a mild contraction in 2024 as rate hikes fully impacted mortgage holders and consumer spending.
Question 5
Theory
Explain comparative advantage and use a numerical example to show how two countries can benefit from trade even when one is more productive in both goods.
Model Answer
Comparative Advantage: A country has comparative advantage in producing a good if it can produce it at a lower opportunity cost than another country. Even if one country has absolute advantage in everything, both can gain from trade by specialising according to comparative advantage.

Example: Australia and Vietnam

Labour hours required per unit:
Wheat (hrs/ton)Clothing (hrs/unit)
Australia26
Vietnam84

Absolute advantage: Australia is more efficient at both (lower hours).

Opportunity costs:
• Australia: 1 wheat costs 2/6 = 0.33 clothing; 1 clothing costs 6/2 = 3 wheat
• Vietnam: 1 wheat costs 8/4 = 2 clothing; 1 clothing costs 4/8 = 0.5 wheat

Comparative advantage:
• Australia has lower opportunity cost in wheat (0.33 < 2) → specialise in wheat
• Vietnam has lower opportunity cost in clothing (0.5 < 3) → specialise in clothing

Gains from trade: Before trade, Australia spent 6 hrs per clothing. By trading 1 wheat (2 hrs) for 1 clothing, it saves 4 hours. Vietnam spent 8 hrs per wheat; by trading 1 clothing (4 hrs) for 1 wheat, it saves 4 hours. Both are better off.

ETC2440 — Business Statistics
ETC2440 · 5 Questions
Question 1
Theory
Distinguish between descriptive statistics and inferential statistics, giving one example of each.
Model Answer
Descriptive Statistics: Methods for organising, summarising, and presenting data in an informative way. They describe the dataset itself — no generalisation beyond the data.

Example: A retail chain calculates that the average (mean) monthly sales across its 50 stores was $450,000 with a standard deviation of $80,000. A histogram shows sales are right-skewed. These describe the existing data but make no claims about other stores or future months.

Inferential Statistics: Methods for making predictions, estimates, or generalisations about a population based on a sample of data. It involves probability and hypothesis testing.

Example: A political pollster surveys 1,200 randomly selected voters and finds 52% support Party A. Using inferential statistics, they construct a 95% confidence interval (e.g., 52% ± 3%) and conclude with 95% confidence that the true population support lies between 49% and 55%. They are inferring something about all voters from a sample.

Key distinction: Descriptive = "What does the data show?" Inferential = "What does the data suggest about the wider world?"
Question 2
Theory
Calculate the mean, median, and mode for the dataset: 12, 15, 18, 18, 22, 25, 30. Which measure is most appropriate for skewed data and why?
Model Answer
Dataset: 12, 15, 18, 18, 22, 25, 30 (n = 7)

Mean:
(12 + 15 + 18 + 18 + 22 + 25 + 30) ÷ 7 = 140 ÷ 7 = 20

Median:
Ordered data has 7 values. The middle value (4th) = 18

Mode:
Most frequent value = 18 (appears twice)

For skewed data:
The median is most appropriate. Unlike the mean, the median is not affected by extreme values (outliers).

Example: House prices in a suburb are typically right-skewed — most houses cluster around $600k–$800k, but a few mansions at $5m+ pull the mean up to $1.2m. The median ($720k) better represents what a "typical" buyer pays. The mean would be misleading.

Similarly, income data is right-skewed (most earn modestly, a few earn millions). Reports use median household income, not mean, for this reason.
Question 3
Theory
A normal distribution has a mean of 100 and standard deviation of 15. What percentage of values fall between 85 and 115? Explain using the empirical rule.
Model Answer
Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7 Rule): For a normal distribution:
• ~68% of data falls within μ ± 1σ
• ~95% of data falls within μ ± 2σ
• ~99.7% of data falls within μ ± 3σ

Given: μ = 100, σ = 15

Range 85 to 115:
85 = 100 − 15 = μ − 1σ
115 = 100 + 15 = μ + 1σ

This range is exactly one standard deviation from the mean.

Therefore, approximately 68% of values fall between 85 and 115.

Practical example: IQ scores are normally distributed with μ = 100, σ = 15. About 68% of the population has an IQ between 85 and 115. Only ~2.5% score above 130 (μ + 2σ) and ~2.5% below 70 (μ − 2σ).

Extension: Between 70 and 130 (μ ± 2σ), approximately 95% of values lie. Between 55 and 145 (μ ± 3σ), approximately 99.7% lie.
Question 4
Theory
Explain Type I and Type II errors in hypothesis testing, using a courtroom analogy.
Model Answer
Hypothesis Testing Setup:
• H₀ (null hypothesis): The default assumption (e.g., "the defendant is innocent")
• H₁ (alternative hypothesis): The claim we seek evidence for (e.g., "the defendant is guilty")

Type I Error (False Positive):
Rejecting H₀ when it is actually true. Concluding there is an effect when there isn't.

Courtroom analogy: Convicting an innocent person. The jury rejects innocence (H₀) when the defendant is actually innocent. The probability of Type I error is α (significance level), typically set at 0.05.

Type II Error (False Negative):
Failing to reject H₀ when it is actually false. Missing a real effect.

Courtroom analogy: Acquitting a guilty person. The jury fails to reject innocence (H₀) when the defendant is actually guilty. The probability of Type II error is β. Statistical power (1 − β) is the probability of correctly rejecting a false null.

Trade-off: Reducing α (stricter standards) increases β, and vice versa. In criminal courts, we set α very low ("beyond reasonable doubt") to minimise Type I errors — better to let 10 guilty go free than convict 1 innocent. In medical screening, we may accept higher α to catch more true cases (minimising Type II errors).
Question 5
Theory
A company surveys 100 customers and finds average satisfaction is 7.2 out of 10 with a standard deviation of 1.5. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the true population mean.
Model Answer
Formula for confidence interval (large sample, z-distribution):
CI = x̄ ± z* × (s ÷ √n)

Given:
• Sample mean (x̄) = 7.2
• Sample standard deviation (s) = 1.5
• Sample size (n) = 100
• Confidence level = 95% → z* = 1.96

Standard error (SE):
SE = s ÷ √n = 1.5 ÷ √100 = 1.5 ÷ 10 = 0.15

Margin of error (ME):
ME = z* × SE = 1.96 × 0.15 = 0.294

95% Confidence Interval:
7.2 ± 0.294 = (6.91, 7.49)

Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the true mean satisfaction score for all customers lies between 6.91 and 7.49. If we repeated this survey many times, 95% of the intervals constructed this way would contain the true population mean.

Note: With n = 100, using the z-distribution is appropriate. For smaller samples (n < 30), the t-distribution should be used instead.

ETW1001 — Business Law
ETW1001 · 5 Questions
Question 1
Theory
What are the three essential elements required for a valid contract? Briefly explain each.
Model Answer
1. Agreement (Offer and Acceptance):
One party must make a clear offer, and the other must accept it unequivocally. The acceptance must mirror the offer ("mirror image rule"). Any variation constitutes a counter-offer, not acceptance. Communication of acceptance is generally required, though silence can constitute acceptance in certain ongoing business relationships.

2. Consideration:
Each party must give something of legal value — money, goods, services, a promise, or forbearance. Consideration must be sufficient (has some value) but need not be adequate (fair market value). Past consideration is not valid. The doctrine of promissory estoppel can enforce promises without consideration in some cases.

3. Intention to Create Legal Relations:
The parties must intend their agreement to be legally binding. In commercial contexts, this is presumed. In social or domestic agreements (e.g., between family members), it is presumed absent unless proven otherwise. Courts examine the language, context, and circumstances to determine intent.
Question 2
Theory
Explain the difference between express terms and implied terms in a contract, giving one example of each.
Model Answer
Express Terms: Stated explicitly by the parties, either orally or in writing. They are the negotiated, agreed-upon provisions of the contract.

Example: A software licence agreement states: "The licence fee is $10,000 per year, payable annually in advance. Support is included for business hours only (9am–5pm AEST)." These are express terms — clearly written and agreed.

Implied Terms: Not explicitly stated but read into the contract by law, custom, or the courts. They fill gaps to make the contract workable or fair.

Example: When you buy a refrigerator from a retailer, the Australian Consumer Law implies a term that the goods are of "acceptable quality" — fit for purpose, free from defects, safe, and durable. Even if the contract says nothing about quality, this term is implied by statute and cannot be excluded in consumer contracts.

Sources of implied terms:
• Statute: Australian Consumer Law, Fair Work Act
• Common law: Terms implied by courts (e.g., duty of care in employment)
• Custom/trade usage: Standard practices in a particular industry
Question 3
Theory
What is negligence? Outline the four elements a plaintiff must prove to succeed in a negligence claim.
Model Answer
Negligence: A tort (civil wrong) where a person breaches their duty of care, causing foreseeable harm to another. It is the most common basis for personal injury and property damage claims.

Four Elements (Donoghue v Stevenson [1932]):

1. Duty of Care: The defendant owed the plaintiff a legal duty to take reasonable care. Established by the "neighbour principle" — you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions reasonably foreseeable as likely to injure your neighbour (anyone closely and directly affected).

2. Breach of Duty: The defendant failed to meet the standard of care expected. The standard is that of a "reasonable person" in the defendant's position. Courts consider the likelihood of harm, severity of potential harm, cost of precautions, and social utility of the defendant's activity.

3. Causation: The breach caused the plaintiff's loss. Two tests:
• Factual causation ("but for"): But for the defendant's breach, would the harm have occurred?
• Legal causation (remoteness): Was the harm a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the breach?

4. Damage: The plaintiff suffered actual loss — physical injury, property damage, psychiatric harm, or pure economic loss (in limited circumstances). Nominal damages are not available in negligence; without loss, there is no claim.
Question 4
Theory
Describe the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) guarantee that goods must be of "acceptable quality" and explain when a consumer can seek a remedy.
Model Answer
Acceptable Quality (ACL s 54): Goods sold to consumers in trade or commerce must be of acceptable quality. This is a statutory guarantee that cannot be excluded. "Acceptable quality" means the goods are:

• Fit for all purposes disclosed or implicit (e.g., a toaster must toast bread)
• Safe, durable, and free from defects (minor defects may be acceptable at low price points)
• Acceptable in appearance and finish
• Reasonably priced in relation to their condition (a $2,000 phone should last longer than a $200 phone)

Quality is assessed objectively — what a reasonable consumer, fully acquainted with the state and condition of the goods, would regard as acceptable.

Remedies:

If goods fail to meet the guarantee, the consumer has rights against the supplier (and sometimes the manufacturer):

• Minor failure: The supplier can choose to repair, replace, or refund.
• Major failure: The consumer chooses the remedy — repair, replacement, or refund. A major failure occurs if the goods would not have been acquired had the consumer known of the problem, or if they are substantially unfit for purpose and cannot be remedied within a reasonable time.

Example: A $1,500 laptop fails after 3 months due to a motherboard defect. This is a major failure. The consumer can demand a full refund or replacement, regardless of the retailer's "no returns" policy.
Question 5
Theory
Distinguish between a company and a sole trader as business structures, noting one advantage and one disadvantage of each.
Model Answer
Sole Trader: An individual operating a business in their own name. No separate legal entity — the business and the person are one.

Advantage: Simplicity and control. No registration fees, no complex reporting, minimal compliance. The owner makes all decisions and keeps all profits.

Disadvantage: Unlimited liability. The owner's personal assets (house, car, savings) are at risk if the business incurs debts or is sued. Bankruptcy of the business means personal bankruptcy.

Company (Pty Ltd): A separate legal entity registered under the Corporations Act 2001. It exists independently of its owners (shareholders).

Advantage: Limited liability. Shareholders' liability is generally limited to the amount unpaid on their shares. Personal assets are protected from business debts (unless personal guarantees are given).

Disadvantage: Complexity and cost. Registration with ASIC, annual review fees, audited financial statements (for large companies), director duties under the Corporations Act, and more onerous tax compliance. Losses cannot be offset against the owner's other personal income.

When to choose: Sole trader for low-risk, small-scale operations. Company for businesses with significant assets, employees, contractual risk, or growth ambitions requiring external investment.

Question 1
Theory
Describe Fayol's five functions of management and explain how they remain relevant in modern organisations.
Model Answer
Henri Fayol (1841–1925) proposed five universal functions of management that still form the backbone of management theory:

1. Planning: Defining goals, establishing strategies, and developing plans to coordinate activities. Modern relevance: Strategic planning, OKRs (Objectives and Key Results), agile sprint planning, scenario analysis.

2. Organising: Arranging work, allocating resources, and assigning tasks. Modern relevance: Organisational design (flat vs hierarchical), remote team structures, cross-functional project teams, resource allocation software.

3. Commanding (Directing): Leading, motivating, and communicating with staff. Modern relevance: Transformational leadership, emotional intelligence, coaching cultures, transparent communication via Slack/Teams.

4. Coordinating: Harmonising activities across departments. Modern relevance: ERP systems, project management tools (Asana, Monday.com), matrix management structures, regular cross-functional stand-ups.

5. Controlling: Monitoring performance, comparing against standards, and taking corrective action. Modern relevance: KPI dashboards, balanced scorecards, real-time analytics, continuous feedback loops (not just annual reviews).

Why they remain relevant: Despite changes in technology and work patterns (remote work, AI, gig economy), the fundamental activities of setting direction, organising resources, leading people, aligning efforts, and monitoring results are timeless. Modern tools enhance execution but don't replace the functions.
Question 2
Theory
Compare and contrast Maslow's hierarchy of needs with Herzberg's two-factor theory as motivation frameworks.
Model Answer
Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs (1943):
A content theory proposing five levels of human needs, arranged hierarchically. Lower needs must be satisfied before higher needs motivate behaviour:
1. Physiological (food, shelter)
2. Safety (security, stability)
3. Social/Belonging (friendship, love)
4. Esteem (recognition, achievement)
5. Self-actualisation (fulfilling potential)

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory (1959):
Distinguishes between hygiene factors (prevent dissatisfaction) and motivators (create satisfaction):

Hygiene factors: Salary, working conditions, company policy, supervision, job security. Their absence causes dissatisfaction; their presence merely prevents it. They do not motivate.

Motivators: Achievement, recognition, meaningful work, responsibility, advancement, growth. These create positive satisfaction and drive high performance.

Key Contrasts:
• Maslow is general (all human needs); Herzberg is workplace-specific.
• Maslow suggests one level must be met before the next; Herzberg says hygiene and motivators operate independently.
• Maslow implies money (physiological) is a basic motivator; Herzberg classifies salary as a hygiene factor (not a true motivator).

Practical implication: Managers must ensure hygiene factors are adequate (fair pay, safe conditions) and then focus on motivators (challenging work, autonomy, recognition) to drive engagement.
Question 3
Theory
What is organisational culture? Describe two ways a strong culture can benefit a company and one potential risk.
Model Answer
Organisational Culture: The shared values, beliefs, assumptions, norms, and practices that shape how members of an organisation think, feel, and behave. It is "the way we do things around here." Edgar Schein described three levels: artefacts (visible behaviours), espoused values (stated norms), and basic underlying assumptions (unconscious beliefs).

Two Benefits of Strong Culture:

1. Alignment and reduced coordination costs: When employees share values and norms, less formal control is needed. Decisions are made consistently without micromanagement. Example: At Netflix, the culture of "freedom and responsibility" means employees are trusted to make big decisions without layers of approval.

2. Employee engagement and retention: A positive, distinctive culture attracts talent and fosters loyalty. Employees who identify with organisational values are more motivated and less likely to leave. Example: Patagonia's environmental mission attracts employees passionate about sustainability, reducing turnover.

One Risk:

Resistance to change and groupthink: A very strong culture can become rigid. "This is how we've always done it" blocks innovation and adaptation. Enron had an aggressive, results-at-all-costs culture that enabled fraud. Strong cultures can also marginalise diverse perspectives, reducing creativity and problem-solving capacity.
Question 4
Theory
Explain the difference between centralised and decentralised organisational structures, giving one advantage of each.
Model Answer
Centralised Structure: Decision-making authority is concentrated at the top of the organisation (senior management / head office). Lower levels implement decisions but have limited autonomy.

Advantage: Consistency and control. Senior leaders ensure all divisions follow the same strategy, standards, and brand identity. Policies are uniform. Useful in crises or highly regulated industries where compliance is critical. Example: Military organisations and banks use centralisation for operational discipline and risk management.

Decentralised Structure: Decision-making authority is distributed to middle and lower-level managers, often close to the customer or operation. Divisions or regions operate with significant autonomy.

Advantage: Responsiveness and empowerment. Local managers can adapt quickly to market conditions, customer needs, and competitive threats without waiting for head office approval. Employees feel empowered, which can boost motivation and innovation. Example: Starbucks gives store managers discretion over local marketing, community engagement, and staffing to suit neighbourhood preferences.

Modern trend: Many organisations adopt a hybrid approach — strategic decisions are centralised, while operational decisions are decentralised. This balances control with agility.
Question 5
Theory
What is the difference between leadership and management? Can a person be an effective leader without being a good manager, and vice versa?
Model Answer
Management: About coping with complexity — planning, budgeting, organising, staffing, controlling, and problem-solving. Managers bring order and predictability. They ask: "Are we doing things right?"

Leadership: About coping with change — setting direction, aligning people, motivating, and inspiring. Leaders create vision and movement. They ask: "Are we doing the right things?"

Key distinction (Kotter):
• Management produces degree of predictability and order
• Leadership produces movement and useful change

Can you be one without the other?

Yes — effective leader, poor manager:
A charismatic founder with a brilliant vision may lack operational discipline. They inspire teams and attract investors but struggle with budgets, deadlines, and process. The company may grow chaotically or burn cash. Example: Some early-stage tech founders excel at product vision but need COOs to handle execution.

Yes — good manager, poor leader:
A highly efficient operations manager may keep processes running smoothly but fail to inspire teams, adapt to market shifts, or articulate a compelling vision. Morale may stagnate; innovation suffers. The team performs adequately but doesn't excel or grow.

Ideal: The most effective executives combine both — they manage resources efficiently AND lead people toward a meaningful future. Organisations need both capabilities, whether in one person or a complementary leadership team.
