Question 1 — SWOT Analysis (Theory + Case)
Theory Case Study
Read the following case study about "GreenLeaf Organics," a small organic grocery store in Melbourne:

GreenLeaf Organics was founded in 2020 by siblings Maya and Raj Patel. They source organic produce directly from local Victorian farms. Their store has built a loyal customer base of health-conscious consumers. Revenue has grown 15% annually. However, they face increasing competition from large supermarket chains (Coles, Woolworths) that now offer organic lines at lower prices. Their single location limits market reach. Consumer trends show growing demand for online grocery delivery and plant-based products.

Required:
  1. Conduct a SWOT analysis for GreenLeaf Organics. Identify at least two factors for each category.
  2. Based on your SWOT, recommend one strategic action the business should take, justifying your choice.
Model Answer

(a) SWOT Analysis for GreenLeaf Organics

Strengths (Internal, Positive):

  • Direct farm relationships: Sourcing directly from local Victorian farms ensures fresh produce, supports local agriculture, and may provide cost advantages by eliminating intermediaries.
  • Loyal customer base: A dedicated following of health-conscious consumers provides stable revenue and word-of-mouth marketing. This brand loyalty acts as a buffer against price competition.
  • Strong growth trajectory: 15% annual revenue growth demonstrates product-market fit and effective operations.

Weaknesses (Internal, Negative):

  • Single location: Limited to one physical store, restricting market reach and creating geographic vulnerability. All revenue depends on foot traffic in one area.
  • Limited economies of scale: As a small independent store, GreenLeaf cannot match the purchasing power or marketing budgets of large chains.
  • Potentially limited online presence: The case mentions consumer trends toward online delivery, suggesting GreenLeaf may be behind in digital transformation.

Opportunities (External, Positive):

  • Growing demand for plant-based products: Consumer trends favoring plant-based diets present an opportunity to expand product lines and attract new customer segments.
  • Online grocery delivery trend: Developing an e-commerce platform with delivery service could significantly expand market reach beyond the physical store's catchment area.
  • Partnership potential: Collaborating with meal-kit services or corporate wellness programs could create new revenue streams.

Threats (External, Negative):

  • Competition from supermarket chains: Coles and Woolworths offering organic lines at lower prices due to economies of scale threatens GreenLeaf's price-sensitive customers.
  • Economic downturn: Organic products are often perceived as premium; during economic hardship, consumers may trade down to conventional products.
  • Supply chain disruptions: Reliance on local farms could be vulnerable to climate events (drought, bushfires) affecting Victorian agriculture.

(b) Strategic Recommendation

Recommended Action: Develop an e-commerce platform with local delivery service and subscription boxes.

Justification:

  1. Addresses the weakness of single location by expanding market reach across Melbourne without the capital expense of new stores.
  2. Capitalizes on the opportunity of growing online grocery demand, meeting customers where their preferences are shifting.
  3. Differentiates from supermarket chains by offering personalized service, curated organic boxes, and farm-to-table storytelling that large chains cannot replicate.
  4. Leverages existing strength of direct farm relationships as a unique selling proposition in marketing.
Question 2 — Break-Even Analysis (Calculation + Graph)
Calculation Graph
GreenLeaf Organics sells organic juice bottles at $8 each. Variable cost per bottle is $4.50. Monthly fixed costs (rent, salaries, utilities) are $7,000.
Required:
  1. Calculate the contribution margin per unit and contribution margin ratio.
  2. Calculate the break-even point in units and in dollars.
  3. If the owner targets a monthly profit of $5,000, how many bottles must be sold?
  4. Calculate the margin of safety if actual monthly sales are 3,000 bottles.
  5. Sketch a break-even chart showing: fixed cost line, total cost line, total revenue line, and label the break-even point.
Model Answer

(a) Contribution Margin

Contribution per unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost = $8.00 − $4.50 = $3.50
CM Ratio = $3.50 / $8.00 = 43.75%

(b) Break-Even Point

BEP (units) = Fixed Costs / Contribution per unit = $7,000 / $3.50 = 2,000 bottles
BEP ($) = Fixed Costs / CM Ratio = $7,000 / 0.4375 = $16,000
Or: 2,000 bottles × $8 = $16,000 ✓

(c) Target Profit Sales

Required sales (units) = (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) / Contribution per unit
= ($7,000 + $5,000) / $3.50 = $12,000 / $3.50 = 3,429 bottles (rounded up)

(d) Margin of Safety

Margin of Safety (units) = Actual Sales − BEP Sales = 3,000 − 2,000 = 1,000 bottles
Margin of Safety (%) = (1,000 / 3,000) × 100% = 33.33%

(e) Break-Even Chart Description

A properly drawn break-even chart would show:

  • Horizontal axis (x-axis): Units sold (0 to 4,000 bottles)
  • Vertical axis (y-axis): Dollars ($0 to $35,000)
  • Fixed Cost line: Horizontal line at $7,000 (constant regardless of output)
  • Total Cost line: Starts at $7,000 on y-axis, slopes upward with gradient = $4.50 per unit
  • Total Revenue line: Starts at origin (0,0), slopes upward with gradient = $8 per unit
  • Break-even point: Intersection of Total Revenue and Total Cost at 2,000 units, $16,000
  • Loss area: Between 0 and 2,000 units (TR below TC)
  • Profit area: Above 2,000 units (TR above TC)

At 3,000 units, the vertical gap between TR and TC represents the profit of $3,500 (1,000 units × $3.50 contribution).

Question 1 — Contract Formation (Theory + Case Analysis)
Theory Case Study
Scenario: Sarah, a café owner, sees an advertisement in a trade magazine: "Commercial espresso machine — $4,500. First come, first served. Contact EspressoCo at sales@espressoco.com.au." Sarah immediately emails EspressoCo stating: "I accept your offer. Please deliver the machine to my café at 123 High Street by Friday." EspressoCo replies: "Thank you for your interest. The machine has already been sold to another buyer."
Required:
  1. Was the advertisement an offer or an invitation to treat? Explain with reference to legal principles and a relevant case.
  2. Did a valid contract exist between Sarah and EspressoCo? Discuss all elements of contract formation.
  3. Assuming a contract was formed, what remedies would be available to Sarah?
Model Answer

(a) Advertisement: Offer or Invitation to Treat?

The advertisement was most likely an invitation to treat, not an offer. In contract law, advertisements are generally treated as invitations to treat because they lack the certainty and intention to be bound required for an offer. The phrase "first come, first served" suggests the seller retains discretion about whom to sell to.

Key case: Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists (Southern) Ltd [1953] — The court held that goods displayed on shelves with price tags were invitations to treat, not offers. The customer's act of taking goods to the counter was the offer, which the cashier could accept or reject.

Similarly, in Partridge v Crittenden [1968], an advertisement for wild birds was held to be an invitation to treat because the seller could not have unlimited stock to satisfy all potential acceptances. Applying this principle, EspressoCo's advertisement invited offers from potential buyers.

(b) Valid Contract Formation

For a valid contract, five elements must be present:

  1. Offer: Sarah's email may be construed as an offer to buy, but since the advertisement was an invitation to treat, no offer existed from EspressoCo. Sarah's email was actually the offer.
  2. Acceptance: EspressoCo did not accept Sarah's offer; instead they rejected it. Under the mailbox rule (Adams v Lindsell [1818]), acceptance is effective upon posting, but rejection is only effective when received.
  3. Consideration: $4,500 from Sarah in exchange for the machine from EspressoCo. Consideration was present but no agreement was reached.
  4. Intention to Create Legal Relations: Presumed in commercial transactions (Edwards v Skyways Ltd [1964]).
  5. Capacity: Both parties are business entities with legal capacity.

Conclusion: No valid contract was formed because EspressoCo never accepted Sarah's offer.

(c) Remedies (if contract existed)

Had a contract been formed, Sarah could seek:

  • Damages: The primary remedy for breach of contract. Sarah could claim the difference between the contract price ($4,500) and the cost of obtaining a substitute machine (expectation interest), or reimbursement of any reliance expenses.
  • Specific Performance: Unlikely for a standard commercial good since damages would be adequate. Specific performance is typically reserved for unique items like land (Co-operative Insurance Society Ltd v Argyll Stores Ltd [1998]).
  • Injunction: Not applicable in this scenario.
Question 2 — Negligence (Theory + Case Application)
Theory
Scenario: David visits a supermarket. A refrigerator unit is leaking water onto the floor. David slips, falls, and breaks his wrist. The supermarket had received a complaint about the leak 30 minutes earlier but had not yet placed warning signs or cleaned the floor.
Required:
  1. Explain the three elements required to establish a claim in negligence.
  2. Apply each element to David's situation.
  3. Discuss any defences the supermarket might raise and whether they would succeed.
Model Answer

(a) Three Elements of Negligence

  1. Duty of Care: The defendant must owe the plaintiff a duty to take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harm. Established by the neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932]: "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour."
  2. Breach of Duty: The defendant's conduct must fall below the standard of care expected of a reasonable person in the circumstances. The standard is objective — what would a reasonable person have done? (Paris v Stepney Borough Council [1951]).
  3. Causation and Damage: The breach must cause the plaintiff's loss. This requires:
    • Factual causation: "But for" the breach, would the harm have occurred? (Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington Hospital [1969])
    • Legal causation (remoteness): The harm must not be too remote. The Wagon Mound test applies (Overseas Tankship v Morts Dock [1961]): was the type of damage foreseeable?

(b) Application to David's Case

  1. Duty of Care: The supermarket owes a duty to its customers to maintain safe premises. This is well-established under occupiers' liability and the general duty of care in negligence. Customers are a foreseeable class of persons, and slipping on wet floors is a foreseeable type of harm.
  2. Breach of Duty: The supermarket breached this duty. A reasonable supermarket would:
    • Immediately place warning signs upon receiving a complaint about a leak
    • Clean or cordon off the affected area
    • Repair the refrigerator or redirect foot traffic

    The supermarket knew of the hazard for 30 minutes and took no action. This falls below the standard of care expected. Australian Safeway Stores v Zaluzna [1987] confirmed that businesses must take reasonable steps to ensure customer safety.

  3. Causation and Damage:
    • Factual causation: But for the water on the floor, David would not have slipped. The breach directly caused the injury.
    • Remoteness: A broken wrist from slipping on a wet floor is a foreseeable type of injury. Not too remote.

Conclusion: David has a strong claim in negligence against the supermarket.

(c) Defences

The supermarket might raise:

  • Contributory Negligence: Argue David was not watching where he was walking. Under the Wrongs Act 1958 (Vic), if David contributed to his own injury, damages may be reduced. However, water on a supermarket floor is not easily visible, and David had no warning. This defence would likely fail or result in minimal reduction.
  • Volenti Non Fit Injuria: Claim David voluntarily assumed the risk. This requires proof David knew of the specific risk and voluntarily accepted it. David was unaware of the leak, so this defence would fail.
  • Obvious Risk: Under s. 13 of the Australian Consumer Law and Fair Trading Act 2012 (Vic), a defendant is not liable for obvious risks. However, a leaking refrigerator is not an obvious risk to a customer who has not been warned.
Question 3 — Australian Consumer Law (Theory)
Theory
James purchases a $2,500 laptop from an electronics retailer. Within two weeks, the battery fails to hold charge, the keyboard becomes unresponsive, and the laptop overheats during normal use. James returns to the retailer who tells him: "Sorry, we don't offer refunds. You'll have to contact the manufacturer."
Required:
  1. Identify which consumer guarantees under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) have been breached.
  2. What remedies is James entitled to under the ACL?
  3. Is the retailer's statement that James must contact the manufacturer legally correct? Explain.
Model Answer

(a) Breached Consumer Guarantees (ACL, Schedule 2, Competition and Consumer Act 2010)

  1. Guarantee as to Acceptable Quality (s 54): Goods must be fit for purpose, free from defects, safe, and durable. A laptop that overheats and has keyboard failure within two weeks is not of acceptable quality.
  2. Guarantee as to Fitness for Purpose (s 55): If a consumer makes known the purpose for which goods are required, they must be reasonably fit for that purpose. A laptop must be fit for general computing purposes.
  3. Guarantee Relating to Supply by Description (s 56): Goods must match any description provided. If advertised as a functional laptop, it must perform as such.
  4. Guarantee as to Repairs and Spare Parts (s 58): The manufacturer must ensure facilities for repair and spare parts are available for a reasonable period.

(b) Remedies Available to James

Under s 259 of the ACL, James has the following remedies against the supplier (the retailer):

  • Minor failure: The retailer may choose to repair, replace, or refund.
  • Major failure: James can choose to:
    • Reject the goods and obtain a full refund or replacement, OR
    • Keep the goods and recover compensation for the reduction in value

A major failure exists if a reasonable consumer would not have acquired the goods had they known about the failure (s 260). Multiple defects in a new $2,500 laptop within two weeks constitutes a major failure.

James is entitled to a full refund or replacement at his choice. He may also claim consequential losses (e.g., lost work, data recovery costs) under s 259(4).

(c) Retailer's Statement

The retailer's statement is incorrect and unlawful under the ACL. Key points:

  • Under s 259, remedies for breach of consumer guarantees are against the supplier (the retailer), not the manufacturer. James purchased from the retailer, making the retailer directly liable.
  • While the manufacturer also has obligations under s 271, James can choose to pursue either the retailer or the manufacturer.
  • The retailer's statement "we don't offer refunds" is a false or misleading representation about consumer rights, which breaches s 29(1)(m) of the ACL.
  • Under s 18, this conduct may also constitute misleading or deceptive conduct.
  • The ACCC can impose penalties for misleading consumers about their statutory rights, and the retailer cannot contract out of consumer guarantees (s 64).

Question 1 — Demand, Supply & Equilibrium (Calculation + Graph)
Calculation Graph
The market for organic coffee beans in Australia is described by:
Demand: Qd = 200 − 4P
Supply: Qs = 20 + 2P

Where Q is in thousands of kg per month and P is in dollars per kg.

Required:
  1. Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity algebraically.
  2. If the government imposes a price ceiling of $25 per kg, calculate the quantity demanded, quantity supplied, and the resulting shortage or surplus.
  3. Sketch a demand-supply diagram showing: the demand curve, supply curve, equilibrium point, price ceiling, and the shortage/surplus area. Label all intercepts.
  4. Calculate the price elasticity of demand at the equilibrium point and interpret your result.
Model Answer

(a) Equilibrium Price and Quantity

At equilibrium, Qd = Qs:

200 − 4P = 20 + 2P
180 = 6P
P* = $30 per kg
Q* = 200 − 4(30) = 200 − 120 = 80 thousand kg
Check: Qs = 20 + 2(30) = 80 ✓

(b) Price Ceiling at P = $25

Qd = 200 − 4(25) = 200 − 100 = 100 thousand kg
Qs = 20 + 2(25) = 20 + 50 = 70 thousand kg
Shortage = Qd − Qs = 100 − 70 = 30 thousand kg

Since $25 is below the equilibrium price of $30, the price ceiling is binding and creates a shortage of 30,000 kg per month.

(c) Demand-Supply Diagram Description

A properly drawn diagram would show:

  • Vertical axis (P): $0 to $60
  • Horizontal axis (Q): 0 to 200 thousand kg
  • Demand curve: Downward sloping from (0, 50) to (200, 0). Intercepts: P-intercept = 50, Q-intercept = 200
  • Supply curve: Upward sloping from (0, −10) starting effectively at Q=20 when P=0. Passes through (80, 30)
  • Equilibrium (E): Labelled at (80, 30)
  • Price ceiling line: Horizontal dashed line at P = $25
  • Shortage: Horizontal distance between Qd (100) and Qs (70) at P = $25, shaded and labelled as "Shortage = 30,000 kg"

(d) Price Elasticity of Demand at Equilibrium

PED = (dQd/dP) × (P/Q)
dQd/dP = −4 (coefficient of P in demand equation)
PED = (−4) × (30/80) = −4 × 0.375 = −1.5
|PED| = 1.5

Interpretation: Demand is elastic (|PED| > 1). A 1% increase in price leads to a 1.5% decrease in quantity demanded. This means total revenue would decrease if price increases (since the percentage drop in quantity demanded exceeds the percentage price rise). The coffee market is responsive to price changes, possibly due to availability of substitutes (regular coffee, tea).

Question 2 — Price Elasticity & Total Revenue (Calculation)
Calculation
A concert venue sells tickets at $80 each and sells 5,000 tickets per show. Market research suggests that if the price is increased to $90, ticket sales will fall to 4,200.
Required:
  1. Calculate the price elasticity of demand using the midpoint (arc elasticity) formula.
  2. Calculate total revenue at both prices and explain the relationship between elasticity and total revenue.
  3. Should the venue increase the ticket price to maximize revenue? Justify your answer.
Model Answer

(a) Arc Elasticity of Demand

Arc PED = [(Q₂ − Q₁) / ((Q₁ + Q₂)/2)] / [(P₂ − P₁) / ((P₁ + P₂)/2)]

% change in Q = (4,200 − 5,000) / ((5,000 + 4,200)/2) = (−800) / 4,600 = −0.1739 or −17.39%
% change in P = (90 − 80) / ((80 + 90)/2) = 10 / 85 = 0.1176 or 11.76%

Arc PED = −0.1739 / 0.1176 = −1.48
|PED| = 1.48

(b) Total Revenue and Elasticity Relationship

TR at $80 = $80 × 5,000 = $400,000
TR at $90 = $90 × 4,200 = $378,000

Since demand is elastic (|PED| > 1), the percentage decrease in quantity demanded (−17.39%) is greater than the percentage increase in price (+11.76%). Therefore, total revenue decreases when price increases. This confirms the elasticity-revenue relationship: when demand is elastic, price and total revenue move in opposite directions.

(c) Pricing Recommendation

The venue should NOT increase the ticket price to $90 because:

  • Total revenue would decrease from $400,000 to $378,000 (a loss of $22,000 per show)
  • The elastic demand indicates consumers are price-sensitive, likely due to substitute entertainment options
  • To maximize revenue, the venue should consider lowering the price, as this would increase total revenue when demand is elastic
  • However, profit maximization also depends on costs. If marginal cost is low, lowering price could increase both revenue and profit
Question 3 — Macroeconomics & Fiscal Policy (Theory)
Theory
Australia's unemployment rate has risen to 6.2% and GDP growth has slowed to 1.5% annually. Inflation remains low at 1.8%, below the RBA's target band of 2–3%.
Required:
  1. Identify the phase of the business cycle Australia is likely experiencing. Explain two characteristics of this phase.
  2. Explain how expansionary fiscal policy could be used to address this situation. Include specific policy tools.
  3. Discuss one potential limitation of expansionary fiscal policy in this context.
Model Answer

(a) Business Cycle Phase

Australia is likely in the recession or trough phase of the business cycle, or at least experiencing a significant slowdown.

Characteristics:

  1. Rising unemployment: At 6.2%, unemployment is above Australia's natural rate (typically 4–5%). During slowdowns, firms reduce production and lay off workers as aggregate demand falls.
  2. Low inflation: At 1.8%, inflation is below the RBA target band. Weak demand means firms cannot raise prices, and there is spare capacity in the economy (negative output gap).

(b) Expansionary Fiscal Policy

Expansionary fiscal policy involves the government increasing aggregate demand through:

  1. Increased government spending (G):
    • Infrastructure projects (roads, railways, renewable energy)
    • Increased funding for healthcare and education
    • Direct cash transfers to households (stimulus payments)

    This directly increases aggregate demand (AD = C + I + G + X − M), creating jobs through the multiplier effect.

  2. Tax cuts:
    • Reducing personal income tax increases disposable income, boosting consumption (C)
    • Reducing corporate tax encourages business investment (I)
    • Targeted tax credits for low-income households have higher marginal propensity to consume

The multiplier effect means the initial increase in spending generates further rounds of income and consumption. For example, if the government spends $10 billion on infrastructure and the marginal propensity to consume is 0.8, the total increase in GDP = $10 billion × (1/0.2) = $50 billion.

(c) Limitation of Expansionary Fiscal Policy

Crowding out: If the government borrows heavily to fund expansionary policy, it may increase interest rates (due to higher demand for loanable funds), which reduces private investment. The extent of crowding out depends on:

  • The state of the economy — in a recession with spare capacity, crowding out is minimal
  • How the spending is financed — borrowing crowds out more than money creation
  • Investor confidence — if business confidence is low, private investment may not respond much to interest rate changes anyway

Another limitation is time lags — recognition lag (identifying the problem), implementation lag (passing legislation), and impact lag (policy taking effect). By the time fiscal policy works, the economy may have already recovered.

Question 1 — Motivation Theory Application (Theory + Case)
Theory Case Study
Case Study: TechFlow Solutions is a software development company experiencing high employee turnover. Exit interviews reveal that employees feel their work is not recognized, career progression is unclear, and their salaries are below market rate. However, employees report that they enjoy the challenging projects and the collaborative team environment.
Required:
  1. Apply Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory to explain the situation at TechFlow Solutions. Identify which factors are causing dissatisfaction and which are providing satisfaction.
  2. Using Vroom's Expectancy Theory, explain why employees might be choosing to leave despite enjoying the work itself.
  3. Recommend three specific actions TechFlow management should take, drawing on the motivation theories discussed.
Model Answer

(a) Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory Application

Hygiene Factors (causing dissatisfaction when absent/inadequate):

  • Salary below market rate: This is a hygiene factor. Inadequate pay creates dissatisfaction but paying market rate only prevents dissatisfaction — it does not create motivation.
  • Lack of recognition: While recognition can be a motivator, the absence of any acknowledgment creates dissatisfaction with company policy/supervision.
  • Unclear career progression: This relates to company policy and administration. Employees feel the organization does not provide structure for advancement.

Motivators (creating satisfaction and motivation):

  • Challenging projects: The work itself is a strong motivator. Employees find the tasks interesting and engaging.
  • Collaborative team environment: This relates to interpersonal relationships and a sense of achievement through teamwork.

Conclusion: TechFlow has strong motivators (good work, good teams) but poor hygiene factors (pay, recognition, progression). Herzberg would argue that fixing hygiene factors will reduce dissatisfaction but won't create motivation — that comes from the motivators already present.

(b) Vroom's Expectancy Theory Application

Vroom's theory states: Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence

Employees at TechFlow may have:

  • High Expectancy: They believe they can perform well (they enjoy challenging projects and work collaboratively).
  • Low Instrumentality: They do not believe that good performance will lead to desired outcomes. If career progression is unclear and recognition is absent, employees perceive no link between effort and reward.
  • High Valence: They value promotions, higher pay, and recognition — but these rewards are not forthcoming.

Since Instrumentality is low (possibly near zero), overall motivation is low despite high ability and valued rewards. Employees rationally conclude that their effort will not be rewarded, so they leave for organizations where the performance-reward link is clearer.

(c) Recommendations

  1. Implement a structured career development program: Create clear pathways from junior to senior roles with defined competencies and timelines. This addresses the hygiene factor of company policy while increasing instrumentality — employees can see that effort leads to advancement.
  2. Introduce a recognition and reward system: Monthly "spotlight" awards, peer recognition platforms, and bonuses tied to project milestones. This addresses the hygiene factor of supervision/recognition and increases the valence of staying.
  3. Conduct a salary benchmarking review and adjust compensation: Bring salaries to market rate. This removes a significant source of dissatisfaction (hygiene factor) and signals that the organization values its employees, increasing organizational commitment.
Question 2 — Leadership Styles (Theory)
Theory
A hospital emergency department is facing a crisis: a major traffic accident has resulted in 15 casualties arriving simultaneously. The department is understaffed, equipment is limited, and decisions about patient prioritization must be made immediately.
Required:
  1. Identify the most appropriate leadership style for this situation using the situational/contingency approach. Justify your choice.
  2. Compare this to the leadership style that would be appropriate for a research and development team designing a new product over a 12-month period. Explain why different situations require different styles.
Model Answer

(a) Appropriate Leadership Style: Autocratic/Directive

In the emergency department crisis, an autocratic (directive) leadership style is most appropriate. This is justified by:

  • Fiedler's Contingency Model: The situation is high-stress, time-pressured, and the task is highly structured (triage protocols exist). Fiedler would suggest task-oriented leadership is most effective when the situation is either very favorable or very unfavorable. A mass casualty incident is an extremely unfavorable situation requiring directive control.
  • Hersey-Blanchard Situational Theory: Staff may be anxious and operating outside their normal capacity (low readiness). A telling/directing style (high task, low relationship) is appropriate when followers are unable and unwilling or insecure.
  • Path-Goal Theory (House): In crises, the path to the goal is unclear and obstacles are extreme. A directive style clarifies expectations, establishes priorities, and removes ambiguity — exactly what stressed staff need.

The leader must make rapid triage decisions, assign roles clearly, and maintain calm authority. Democratic consultation would waste precious time and increase confusion.

(b) R&D Team: Transformational/Democratic Style

For an R&D team over 12 months, a transformational or democratic (participative) style is more appropriate:

  • Transformational leadership: Inspires creativity, encourages innovation, and empowers team members to experiment. This aligns with the need for breakthrough thinking in product design.
  • Democratic style: Team members are likely highly skilled experts (high readiness per Hersey-Blanchard). Participative decision-making leverages their expertise and increases buy-in.
  • Path-Goal: A supportive or participative style works when tasks are complex, non-routine, and followers are competent. The leader's role is to remove obstacles and provide resources, not to dictate solutions.

Why Different Situations Require Different Styles:

Contingency theory demonstrates that no single leadership style is universally effective. The emergency department requires speed, clarity, and control — conditions where autocratic leadership excels. The R&D team requires creativity, expertise integration, and intrinsic motivation — conditions where participative/transformational leadership excels. Effective leaders diagnose the situation (follower readiness, task structure, time pressure, stakes) and adapt their style accordingly.

Calculation

MR = d(TR)/dQ = d(80Q − 2Q²)/dQ = 80 − 4Q

MC = d(TC)/dQ = d(20Q + 100)/dQ = 20

(b) Profit-Maximizing Output

Profit (π) = TR − TC = (80Q − 2Q²) − (20Q + 100) = −2Q² + 60Q − 100

= −2(225) + 900 − 100
= −450 + 900 − 100 = $350 thousand

d²π/dQ² = −4
