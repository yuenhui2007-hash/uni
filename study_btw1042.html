1. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1: Which of the following is an essential element required for the formation of a valid contract?
  • A) Registration with a government body
  • B) Intention to create legal relations
  • C) A written document signed by both parties
  • D) Payment of a stamp duty
Show Answer
Answer: B
Essential elements for a valid contract under common law include: (1) offer, (2) acceptance, (3) consideration, (4) intention to create legal relations, (5) certainty, and (6) capacity. Writing is not always required except for specific contracts like land sales under the Statute of Frauds.
Q2: What does the doctrine of promissory estoppel prevent a party from doing?
  • A) Terminating a contract without notice
  • B) Going back on a promise where the promisee has relied on it to their detriment
  • C) Refusing to perform a contract due to frustration
  • D) Assigning contractual rights to a third party
Show Answer
Answer: B
Promissory estoppel (Waltons Stores v Maher) prevents a promisor from retracting a promise when the promisee has relied on it to their detriment, even without consideration.
Q3: Under the ACL, a term in a standard form consumer contract is void if it is:
  • A) Negotiated individually between the parties
  • B) Transparent and expressed in plain language
  • C) Unfair
  • D) Limited to a fixed term of 12 months
Show Answer
Answer: C
Under ACL s 23, an unfair term in a standard form consumer or small business contract is void. A term is unfair if it causes significant imbalance, is not reasonably necessary to protect legitimate interests, and would cause detriment.
Q4: Which best describes the tort of negligence?
  • A) Intentional interference with a person's goods
  • B) A civil wrong involving breach of a duty of care causing foreseeable harm
  • C) Defamation through spoken false statements
  • D) Strict liability for defective products regardless of fault
Show Answer
Answer: B
Negligence requires: (1) duty of care, (2) breach, (3) causation (factual + legal), and (4) foreseeable damage. Not an intentional tort.
Q5: Under the Partnership Act 1958 (Vic), which is TRUE?
  • A) Partners have limited liability for partnership debts
  • B) Each partner can bind the partnership in the ordinary course of business
  • C) Partnerships must be registered with ASIC to be valid
  • D) A partnership is a separate legal entity distinct from its partners
Show Answer
Answer: B
Partners are agents of the partnership (s 6). Partnerships are NOT separate legal entities, partners have unlimited joint and several liability, and registration is not required.
Q6: What is the legal effect of breaching a condition?
  • A) The innocent party can only claim damages
  • B) The innocent party may terminate and claim damages
  • C) The contract becomes void ab initio
  • D) Both parties are automatically discharged
Show Answer
Answer: B
A condition is an essential term; breach allows termination (repudiation) + damages. Warranty breach = damages only.
Q7: A director's duty to act in good faith is found in:
  • A) s 180
  • B) s 181
  • C) s 182
  • D) s 588G
Show Answer
Answer: B
s 181 = good faith & proper purpose. s 180 = care & diligence. s 182 = improper use of position. s 588G = insolvent trading.
Q8: Remedies for misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18 ACL include:
  • A) Criminal imprisonment only
  • B) Damages, injunctions, and rescission
  • C) Only a public apology
  • D) Automatic termination of the director's appointment
Show Answer
Answer: B
s 18 ACL (civil) remedies: damages, injunctions, rescission, corrective advertising orders. Criminal penalties apply under separate provisions.
Q9: Frustration in contract law applies when:
  • A) A party deliberately refuses to perform
  • B) An unforeseen event renders performance impossible or radically different
  • C) One party suffers financial difficulty
  • D) The contract was entered under duress
Show Answer
Answer: B
Frustration discharges a contract when a supervening event (not caused by either party) makes performance impossible or radically different.
Q10: Which consumer guarantee applies to SERVICES under the ACL?
  • A) Guarantee of acceptable quality
  • B) Guarantee of due care and skill
  • C) Guarantee of fitness for a particular purpose for goods only
  • D) Guarantee of clear title
Show Answer
Answer: B
s 60 ACL: services rendered with due care and skill. Acceptable quality, clear title = goods guarantees (ss 54, 51).

2. How to Write a Business Law Essay

The ILAC Method — Your Essay Blueprint

Every business law problem question should follow the ILAC structure. This is how examiners expect you to analyse legal problems.

LetterMeaningWhat to Write
IIssueIdentify the legal question(s) raised by the facts. Be specific. E.g., "Did Bob breach his duty of care to Alice?"
LLawState the relevant legal rule(s) with statutory references and case law. E.g., "Under Donoghue v Stevenson, a duty of care arises when..."
AApplicationApply the law to the facts. This is the MOST IMPORTANT part. Use the facts given and show how the law fits.
CConclusionAnswer the issue directly. E.g., "Therefore, Bob is likely liable for negligence."

Essay Structure for Problem Questions

Introduction (1 paragraph)
Briefly identify the parties, the dispute, and the areas of law involved. Map out what you'll discuss. Do NOT give conclusions here.
Body — One ILAC per Issue (2-4 paragraphs each)
Each issue gets its own ILAC. Start with the most central issue. Use headings for clarity. Cite cases and statutes. Explain why a case applies, not just that it applies.
Conclusion (1 paragraph)
Summarise your findings. State the likely outcome for each party. Mention any remedies or defences that may reduce liability.

Essay Writing Tips

Do's
  • Use signposting: "The first issue is...", "Turning to...", "In conclusion..."
  • Always cite both statute AND case law where possible
  • Use the facts — don't just describe the law, show how it applies
  • Consider counter-arguments and defences
  • Write in third person (no "I think", "I believe")
  • Use past tense for facts, present tense for law
Don'ts
  • Don't write everything you know about a topic — answer the question
  • Don't just list cases — explain their relevance
  • Don't ignore the Application step — it's where marks are earned
  • Don't use bullet points in essays — use paragraphs
  • Don't leave issues unresolved — always reach a conclusion

Key Phrases to Use

  • "It is well established that..." (for settled law)
  • "The High Court held in [Case] that..." (when citing authority)
  • "On the facts, [Party] appears to have..." (applying law)
  • "Conversely, [Party] may argue that..." (counter-argument)
  • "It is therefore likely/unlikely that..." (conclusion)
  • "In the alternative, if the court finds..." (secondary position)

3.3 Vitiating Factors

Misrepresentation
A false statement of fact inducing contract. Types: fraudulent (Derry v Peek), negligent (Esso v Mardon), innocent. Remedies: rescission + damages (fraudulent/negligent).
Duress
Illegitimate pressure vitiating consent. Types: threats to person, goods, economic duress (Universe Tankships v ITWF). Remedy: rescission.
Undue Influence
Exploitation of a special relationship of trust. Presumed in fiduciary relationships (Johnson v Buttress). Remedy: rescission.
Unconscionability
Taking advantage of a special disability (Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio). Requires: (1) special disability, (2) knowledge, (3) unfair exploitation. Remedy: rescission or equitable compensation.

3.4 Discharge of Contract

  • Performance: Complete and exact performance (Cutter v Powell — substantial performance exception)
  • Agreement: Mutual discharge, variation, or novation
  • Breach: Actual breach or anticipatory breach (Hochster v De La Tour)
  • Frustration: Supervening event making performance impossible (Taylor v Caldwell; Davis Contractors v Fareham). Effects: contract discharged prospectively; parties may recover money under Fibrosa principle.

4. Tort Law — Negligence

4.1 Elements of Negligence

The Four-Step TestCritical
  1. Duty of Care — Was there a duty owed? Apply Donoghue v Stevenson neighbour principle. Modern test: reasonable foreseeability + salient features (Sullivan v Moody; Modbury Triangle v Anzil).
  2. Breach of Duty — Was the standard of care breached? Objective test: what would a reasonable person do? (Paris v Stepney BC; Bolton v Stone)
  3. Causation —
    • Factual causation: "But for" the breach, would the damage have occurred? (Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington HMC)
    • Legal causation (remoteness): Was the damage a reasonably foreseeable kind? (Overseas Tankship v Morts Dock — The Wagon Mound)
  4. Damage — Actual loss or damage suffered. Must be foreseeable type.

4.2 Defences to Negligence

DefenceDescriptionKey Case
Contributory NegligencePlaintiff failed to take reasonable care for own safety; damages reduced proportionallyPodrebersek v Australian Iron & Steel
Volenti Non Fit InjuriaPlaintiff voluntarily assumed the riskICI v Shatwell
Obvious RiskRisk was obvious to a reasonable person (Civil Liability Acts)State legislation

4.3 Negligent Misstatement

Requirements (Esanda Finance v Peat Marwick; Perre v Apand)
  1. Reasonable foreseeability of reliance
  2. Special relationship / proximity between parties
  3. Knowledge of specific purpose and known reliance
  4. Assumption of responsibility

5. Australian Consumer Law

5.1 Misleading or Deceptive Conduct (s 18)

Elements
  1. Conduct in trade or commerce
  2. Conduct that is misleading or deceptive or likely to mislead/deceive
  3. No need to prove intention, reliance, or loss for contravention
  4. For remedies: causation and loss must be shown
Remedies
Damages, injunctions, rescission, compensatory orders, corrective advertising. Key cases: Google v ACCC (search results); ACCC v TPG (fine print).

5.2 Unfair Contract Terms (s 23)

Test for Unfairness
A term is unfair if it: (a) causes significant imbalance; (b) is not reasonably necessary to protect legitimate interests; (c) would cause detriment. Must be in a standard form contract for consumer or small business.
Examples of Unfair Terms
One-sided termination rights, excessive penalties, broad indemnities, automatic renewals without notice, unilateral variation clauses.

6.2 Insolvent Trading (s 588G)

Elements of Breach
  1. Person is a director when debt is incurred
  2. Company is insolvent or becomes insolvent by incurring the debt
  3. There are reasonable grounds for suspecting insolvency
  4. Director fails to prevent debt being incurred
Defences (s 588H)
  1. Reasonable grounds to expect solvency
  2. Reliance on competent person's advice
  3. Took all reasonable steps to prevent debt
  4. Illness prevented participation in management
Safe Harbour (s 588GA)
Protects directors pursuing a course of action reasonably likely to lead to a better outcome than immediate administration/liquidation.

6.3 Remedies for Breach of Directors' Duties

  • Civil penalties: Up to $1.05M per contravention (or 3x benefit gained / loss avoided)
  • Disqualification: From managing corporations
  • Compensation orders: Under s 1317H to the company
  • Statutory derivative action: Shareholders can sue on behalf of company (s 236)
  • Criminal liability: Under ss 184 if dishonesty or recklessness proven

7. Partnership Law

Definition (Partnership Act 1958 (Vic) s 1)
"The relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit."
Key Features
  • Not a separate legal entity — partners are personally liable
  • Agency: Each partner can bind the firm (s 6)
  • Liability: Unlimited, joint and several for partnership debts
  • Fiduciary duties: Partners owe each other duties of good faith, not to compete, and to account for profits
  • Registration: Not required for validity (but recommended for limited partnerships)

8. Detailed Case Studies

Case Study 1: Contract Formation — Offer & Acceptance
Facts
Alice advertises her car for sale at $15,000. Bob emails offering $12,000. Alice replies she will accept $14,000. Bob says "I'll think about it." The next day, Alice sells the car to Charlie for $14,500. Bob then emails accepting $14,000. Alice refuses.
Issue
Is there a binding contract between Alice and Bob?
Law
  • An offer must be distinguished from an invitation to treat (Pharmaceutical Society v Boots)
  • A counter-offer destroys the original offer (Hyde v Wrench)
  • Acceptance must be communicated and unequivocal
Application
Alice's advertisement was an invitation to treat, not an offer. Bob's $12,000 was an offer. Alice's $14,000 was a counter-offer, which rejected Bob's offer. Bob did not accept. His later "acceptance" of $14,000 was an offer that Alice was free to reject. No contract was formed.
Conclusion
No binding contract. Alice was entitled to sell to Charlie. Bob has no remedy.
Case Study 2: Negligence — Duty of Care & Breach
Facts
Dr Chen operates on Paul's knee. During surgery, Dr Chen leaves a surgical instrument inside Paul's leg. Paul develops a serious infection requiring further surgery and months of rehabilitation.
Issue
Is Dr Chen liable for negligence?
Law
  • Doctors owe patients a duty of care (Rogers v Whitaker)
  • Standard of care: reasonable medical practitioner in same field (Bolam test modified in Australia by Rogers v Whitaker)
  • "But for" causation and remoteness apply
Application
Duty: Clearly established doctor-patient relationship.
Breach: Leaving a surgical instrument inside a patient falls well below the standard of any reasonable surgeon. This is not a matter of professional judgment but a fundamental error.
Causation: But for the instrument, Paul would not have developed the infection. The infection is a direct and foreseeable consequence.
Damage: Physical injury, pain, further surgery costs, lost earnings.
Conclusion
Dr Chen is liable for negligence. Paul can claim damages for medical expenses, lost income, pain and suffering, and future care costs.
Case Study 3: Directors' Duties — Conflict of Interest
Facts
Sarah is a director of TechCo Pty Ltd. Without disclosing to the board, Sarah arranges for TechCo to purchase software from her husband's company at 30% above market rate. The board approves the transaction believing it to be arms-length.
Issue
What duties has Sarah breached and what are the consequences?
Law
  • s 181: Good faith and proper purpose — directors must act in the company's best interests
  • s 182: Improper use of position — cannot use position to gain advantage for self/others
  • s 191: Material personal interest must be disclosed
  • Common law fiduciary duty: avoid conflicts of interest (Regal v Gulliver)
Application
Sarah failed to disclose her material personal interest (s 191). She used her position to benefit her husband's business at TechCo's expense (s 182). She did not act in good faith or for a proper purpose (s 181). The transaction was not in TechCo's best interests.
Conclusion
Sarah breached ss 181, 182, and 191. Consequences: civil penalties, disqualification, compensation to TechCo for the overpayment. Shareholders may bring a derivative action (s 236).
Case Study 4: ACL — Misleading & Deceptive Conduct
Facts
FastFitness Gym advertises "Unlimited Access — $0 Joining Fee!" in large print. In tiny print at the bottom, the contract requires a 24-month minimum term with a $500 cancellation fee. Emma signs up based on the advertisement but wants to cancel after 3 months.
Issue
Has FastFitness breached the ACL? Is the cancellation term enforceable?
Law
  • s 18 ACL: Prohibits misleading or deceptive conduct in trade or commerce
  • s 23 ACL: Unfair terms in standard form consumer contracts are void
  • ACCC v TPG: Fine print cannot cure a misleading headline
Application
The advertisement is misleading because the prominent claim "$0 Joining Fee" distracts from the onerous 24-month lock-in and $500 cancellation fee (ACCC v TPG). The cancellation clause in the standard form contract may be an unfair term under s 23: it causes significant imbalance, is not necessary to protect FastFitness's legitimate interests, and would cause detriment to Emma.
Conclusion
FastFitness likely breached s 18. The cancellation term may be void as unfair under s 23. Emma could seek rescission, damages, and an order that the unfair term is not enforceable.
Case Study 5: Insolvent Trading
Facts
BuildCo Pty Ltd owes $500,000 to creditors and cannot pay. Director Mike knows the company is insolvent but continues to order $100,000 of building materials on credit, hoping a big contract will save the business. The contract falls through and BuildCo goes into liquidation.
Issue
Has Mike breached s 588G? What defences might he raise?
Law
  • s 588G: Directors must prevent insolvent trading
  • Elements: director, debt incurred, insolvency, reasonable grounds to suspect, failure to prevent
  • Defences: s 588H (reasonable expectation of solvency, reliance on advice, reasonable steps, illness)
  • Safe harbour: s 588GA
Application
Mike was a director when the $100,000 debt was incurred. BuildCo was insolvent (cannot pay debts as they fall due). Mike knew of the insolvency — there were reasonable grounds to suspect. He failed to prevent the debt. No defence under s 588H applies: he had no reasonable grounds to expect solvency (he knew the company was broke), and he took no reasonable steps to prevent the debt. Safe harbour (s 588GA) does not apply because continuing to trade on hope without a developed plan is not "reasonably likely to lead to a better outcome."
Conclusion
Mike breached s 588G. He faces civil penalties, disqualification, and a compensation order for the $100,000 debt. He may also face criminal liability under s 184 if dishonesty is proven.

Final Exam Tips
  • Time management: Allocate time per question based on marks. A 20-mark question = ~30 minutes.
  • Read the question twice before starting. Identify who is asking what.
  • Use headings — examiners award marks for clear structure.
  • Always conclude — even if uncertain, give your best assessment.
  • Cite statutes precisely — "s 18 Australian Consumer Law" not just "the ACL".
  • Case names in italics — Donoghue v Stevenson, not Donoghue v Stevenson.
