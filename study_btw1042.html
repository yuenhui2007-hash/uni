Monash B2026 Accountancy Exam Study Guide

Multiple Choice Questions

Q1: Which of the following is an essential element required for the formation of a valid contract?
  • A) Registration with a government body
  • B) Intention to create legal relations
  • C) A written document signed by both parties
  • D) Payment of a stamp duty
Show Answer
Answer: B
Essential elements for a valid contract under common law include offer, acceptance, consideration, intention to create legal relations, certainty, and capacity. Writing is not always required (except for specific contracts like land sales under the Statute of Frauds).
Q2: In contract law, what does the doctrine of promissory estoppel prevent a party from doing?
  • A) Terminating a contract without notice
  • B) Going back on a promise where the promisee has relied on it to their detriment
  • C) Refusing to perform a contract due to frustration
  • D) Assigning contractual rights to a third party
Show Answer
Answer: B
Promissory estoppel (central to the High Court's decision in Waltons Stores v Maher) prevents a promisor from retracting a promise when the promisee has relied on it to their detriment, even if no consideration was given for the promise.
Q3: Under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL), a term in a standard form consumer contract is void if it is:
  • A) Negotiated individually between the parties
  • B) Transparent and expressed in plain language
  • C) Unfair
  • D) Limited to a fixed term of 12 months
Show Answer
Answer: C
Under ACL s 23, an unfair term in a standard form consumer or small business contract is void. A term is unfair if it causes significant imbalance, is not reasonably necessary to protect legitimate interests, and would cause detriment.
Q4: Which of the following best describes the tort of negligence?
  • A) Intentional interference with a person's goods
  • B) A civil wrong involving breach of a duty of care causing foreseeable harm
  • C) Defamation through spoken false statements
  • D) Strict liability for defective products regardless of fault
Show Answer
Answer: B
Negligence requires: (1) a duty of care owed by the defendant, (2) breach of that duty, (3) causation (both factual and legal), and (4) foreseeable damage. It is not an intentional tort and does not require malice.
Q5: In a partnership under the Partnership Act 1958 (Vic), which of the following is TRUE?
  • A) Partners have limited liability for partnership debts
  • B) Each partner can bind the partnership in the ordinary course of business
  • C) Partnerships must be registered with ASIC to be valid
  • D) A partnership is a separate legal entity distinct from its partners
Show Answer
Answer: B
Partners are agents of the partnership; each partner can bind the firm in the ordinary course of business (s 6). Partnerships are not separate legal entities, and partners generally have unlimited joint and several liability. Registration is not required for validity.
Q6: What is the legal effect of a breach of a condition in a contract?
  • A) The innocent party can only claim damages
  • B) The innocent party may terminate the contract and claim damages
  • C) The contract becomes void ab initio
  • D) Both parties are automatically discharged from all obligations
Show Answer
Answer: B
A condition is an essential term; its breach gives the innocent party the right to terminate (repudiation) and sue for damages. In contrast, breach of a warranty only entitles the innocent party to damages, not termination.
Q7: Under the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth), a company director's duty to act in good faith is found in:
  • A) s 180
  • B) s 181
  • C) s 182
  • D) s 588G
Show Answer
Answer: B
Section 181 requires directors and officers to exercise their powers and discharge their duties in good faith in the best interests of the corporation and for a proper purpose. Section 180 covers care and diligence; s 182 covers improper use of position; s 588G deals with insolvent trading.
Q8: Which of the following is a remedy available for misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18 of the ACL?
  • A) Criminal imprisonment only
  • B) Damages, injunctions, and rescission of the contract
  • C) Only a public apology from the corporation
  • D) Automatic termination of the director's appointment
Show Answer
Answer: B
Section 18 ACL prohibits misleading or deceptive conduct in trade or commerce. Remedies include damages, injunctions, rescission, orders for corrective advertising, and compensatory orders. It is a civil provision; criminal penalties apply under separate provisions for false representations.
Q9: The concept of 'frustration' in contract law applies when:
  • A) A party deliberately refuses to perform the contract
  • B) An unforeseen event occurs, without the parties' fault, rendering performance impossible or radically different
  • C) One party suffers financial difficulty in fulfilling the contract
  • D) The contract was entered into under duress
Show Answer
Answer: B
Frustration discharges a contract when a supervening event, not caused by either party and not contemplated by the contract, makes performance impossible or something radically different from what was agreed (e.g., destruction of subject matter, government prohibition).
Q10: Under the ACL, which of the following consumer guarantees applies to services?
  • A) Guarantee of acceptable quality
  • B) Guarantee of due care and skill
  • C) Guarantee of fitness for a particular purpose for goods only
  • D) Guarantee of clear title
Show Answer
Answer: B
Under ACL s 60, services must be rendered with due care and skill. Acceptable quality, clear title, and undisturbed possession are guarantees that apply to goods (ss 54, 51, 52). Services also have guarantees as to reasonable time and fitness for a disclosed purpose.

Subjective Questions

Q: Explain the doctrine of consideration and discuss whether it is always necessary for the enforcement of a promise under Australian contract law. Contract Formation
Answer
Consideration is something of value (a benefit or detriment) exchanged between parties to a contract; it is a fundamental requirement for enforceability of simple contracts. The promisee must provide consideration for the promisor's promise, and past consideration is generally insufficient (Roscorla v Thomas). However, consideration is not always necessary. Promissory estoppel, recognised in Waltons Stores v Maher and developed in Commonwealth v Verwayen, may enforce a promise where the promisee has relied on it to their detriment, even absent consideration. Additionally, deeds are enforceable without consideration because of their formal execution. Recent academic debate questions whether consideration remains a meaningful threshold or an unnecessary formality in modern contract law.
Q: A company director approves a transaction that benefits their spouse's business at the expense of the company. Identify the statutory duties breached and outline the potential consequences under the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth). Corporations Law
Answer
The director likely breaches s 181 (good faith and proper purpose), s 182 (improper use of position), and s 183 (improper use of information) of the Corporations Act. Section 181 requires directors to act in the best interests of the company; s 182 prohibits using their position to gain an advantage for themselves or someone else or to cause detriment to the company. Consequences include civil penalties of up to $1.05 million per contravention (or three times the benefit gained), disqualification from managing corporations, compensation orders under s 1317H, and potential criminal liability under ss 184 if dishonesty is proven. ASIC may pursue civil penalty proceedings, and shareholders may bring a statutory derivative action under s 236.
Q: Distinguish between a condition, a warranty, and an intermediate term in a contract. Explain the remedies available for breach of each. Contract Terms
Answer
A condition is an essential term going to the root of the contract; breach allows the innocent party to terminate and claim damages (Associated Newspapers v Bancks). A warranty is a less important, subsidiary term; breach only entitles the innocent party to damages, not termination (Bettini v Gye). An intermediate (or innominate) term does not fit neatly into either category; the remedy depends on the severity of the breach. If the consequences are serious and go to the root of the contract, the innocent party may terminate; otherwise, only damages are available (Hong Kong Fir Shipping v Kawasaki). This approach provides flexibility but introduces uncertainty at the time of contracting.
Q: Discuss the liability of a company for the torts committed by its employees during the course of employment. Refer to the relevant legal principles and case law. Torts & Agency
Answer
A company can be vicariously liable for torts committed by employees acting in the course of employment under the doctrine of respondereat superior (let the employer answer). The key test is whether the employee's conduct was authorised or a wrongful mode of performing an authorised act (Bugge v Brown; Deatons v Flew). The 'close connection' test from Lister v Hesley Hall (UK) and adopted in Australia determines whether the tort was so closely connected to employment that it is fair to impose liability. Companies may also be directly liable for negligence in hiring, training, or supervising employees. Defences include that the employee was on a 'frolic of their own' (acting outside the scope of employment), which breaks the chain of vicarious liability.
Q: Explain the concept of insolvent trading under s 588G of the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth). What defences are available to a director accused of allowing a company to trade while insolvent? Corporate Insolvency
Answer
Section 588G imposes a duty on directors to prevent a company from incurring debts if there are reasonable grounds to suspect insolvency. A director breaches the provision if: (1) they are a director at the time the debt is incurred, (2) the company is insolvent or becomes insolvent by incurring the debt, and (3) there are reasonable grounds for suspecting insolvency. Defences under s 588H include: (a) the director had reasonable grounds to expect, and did expect, that the company was solvent (often relying on financial information); (b) the director reasonably relied on information from a competent and reliable person indicating solvency; (c) the director took all reasonable steps to prevent the debt from being incurred; or (d) due to illness, the director did not take part in management at that time. The safe harbour provision (s 588GA) may also protect directors who are pursuing a course of action reasonably likely to lead to a better outcome for the company than immediate administration or liquidation.
